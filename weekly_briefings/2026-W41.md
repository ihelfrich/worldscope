## Macro: how the picture moved

The dominant macro development this week was not a data release but a yield. The 10-year Treasury ended the week at 5.28 percent and the 30-year at 5.67 percent, while the Federal funds rate sits at 3.88 percent and SOFR at 3.87 percent. The 10-year-minus-2-year spread is now +47 basis points after spending the better part of two years inverted. Disinversion is complete. That spread, in the current context, is not signaling recovery confidence -- it is signaling that investors demand a meaningful term premium to hold long-dated U.S. paper while Congress borrows at a pace of roughly $1.9 trillion per year. Tim Taylor's Conversable Economist ran a piece Friday making precisely this argument: that FY2026 deficit spending of $1.9 trillion out of $7.4 trillion total outlays is structurally inconsistent with the current rate path, and that revenue increases are unavoidable.

The September FOMC minutes, released Wednesday, almost certainly contributed to that day's equity selloff. Prediction markets are pricing exactly zero probability of any cut -- 25 or 50 basis points -- at the October 29 meeting. The Fed is holding while long-end yields drift higher, the real-economy consequence being that mortgage rates remain constraining and the refinancing cycle for corporate debt issued at near-zero rates during 2020-2021 continues to grind.

WTI crude closed the week at $96.24 per barrel (FRED, as of October 6). EUR/USD sits at 1.1259 and USD/JPY at 157.81 to 158.03 -- the yen remains structurally weak, representing an ongoing divergence from the Bank of Japan's slow normalization path relative to where U.S. rates have settled. Gold gained about 1 percent on the week. Natural gas (UNG) rose 5.3 percent, driven by both Hurricane Isaias landfall preparations and early positioning for the European winter -- relevant context given Russia's energy ceasefire offer.

Unemployment held at 4.2 percent and nonfarm payrolls at 159,044 thousand as of September. The Fed's balance sheet is at $6.75 trillion. Nothing in the underlying data justifies imminent easing.

## Markets: rotation and dispersion

The week's market structure was revealing. Three up days (Sunday +0.74%, Monday +0.39%, Tuesday +0.56%), a sharp Wednesday reversal (-0.60% on S&P, -1.14% on Russell 2000, -1.45% on MSCI EAFE), a muted Thursday (-0.18%), and a Friday recovery (+0.27%). Net: the S&P 500 gained 0.83 percent from 769.64 to 776.05. But the cross-section story is China.

FXI, the China large-cap ETF, gained 3.4 percent on the week -- outpacing the S&P by more than four to one. The pattern was choppy (Sunday -2.15%, Monday +1.63%, Tuesday -0.56%, Wednesday -1.18%, Thursday +0.03%, Friday +2.57%), with Golden Week ending and the Friday gap suggesting investors came back to China positioning after the holiday. Caixin coverage this week documented two structural dynamics: Chinese firms actively gutting middle management as AI reshapes job structures, and pressure on local government financing vehicles (LGFVs) to abandon "fake" reform and pursue real balance-sheet cleanup. Both are deflationary in the near term and meaningful for the medium-term investment thesis.

Small-cap underperformed throughout the week. The Russell 2000 finished at 278.95, essentially flat with the week's open but down meaningfully from Tuesday's high of 283. Small-caps are rate-sensitive in a way that large-caps, flush with cash, are not. At 5.28 percent on the 10-year, small-cap credit costs are genuinely constraining.

Long-end Treasuries sold off (TLT -0.32% on the week), consistent with the fiscal story above. High-yield credit (HYG) remained essentially unchanged, and VIX fell from 17.40 to 16.99 -- the market's volatility signal remains calm even as geopolitical noise intensifies. The calmness of VIX should be read against the backdrop of genuinely elevated term premium in rates: the risk is being priced in duration, not in volatility. Bitcoin fell 2.1 percent on the week (BITO from 11.28 to 11.04), consistent with the risk-off Wednesday sessions hitting crypto harder.

## Sanctions and legal

The week's headline sanctions action was the EU's agreement on its largest-ever Russia sanctions expansion: more than 1,500 individuals and entities added in a single package. This is a quantitative escalation intended to close shadow-fleet, technology-transfer, and procurement circumvention channels that have been documented over two years of sanctions implementation.

On the same day (Wednesday, October 8), OFAC issued Iran-related designations, a new Iran-related General License, and an amended Russia-related General License (GL No. 1 under 31 CFR Part 587). The simultaneous Iran and Russia actions are not coincidental: both theaters are in active diplomatic contact with Washington, and OFAC is the instrument that makes sanctions available as a bargaining chip. Russia's request to have sanctions lifted in exchange for an energy ceasefire is, in this light, a direct response to the cumulative pressure -- but the EU expansion the same day signals that the Western coalition is not yet ready to use that chip.

At the Court of International Trade, tariff dockets continue: Freestyle World v. United States and Wabtec Corp. v. United States were active this week. FARA registered a new Whistleblower Awards Pilot Program at the Criminal Division. The Department of Energy obligated $43.46 billion to Sandia National Labs and $34.66 billion to Consolidated Nuclear Security in large management-and-operating contract awards captured in USASpending data -- both nuclear weapons enterprise continuations rather than new starts.

## Geopolitical signal

The week's most consequential geopolitical fact is that negotiations between Ukraine, Washington, and Europe opened in Miami at the same time that Russia killed 33 civilians on city buses in Kramatorsk and Ukrainian drones attacked a Bashkortostan refinery for the first time. These are not contradictory signals; they are the structure of a conflict that both sides are trying to end on their own terms and are simultaneously trying to improve their position in. The energy dimension is central: Russia's offer to stop striking Ukrainian energy infrastructure in exchange for sanctions relief, and Washington's parallel discussions with Moscow about potential gas sales to Europe through a revived Nord Stream, suggest that energy is the one dimension on which the parties can credibly transact.

The Ukraine theater FIRMS data shows persistent thermal anomalies near Rivne (50.5°N, 26.3°E), a critical rail junction and energy hub in western Ukraine, through this week. This is consistent with the Ukrainian internal press reporting that Russia has deployed "mustachioed Shaheds" (modified drones with targeting antennae) specifically against high-voltage transmission lines. Three Kyiv districts activated resilience centers due to power outages following Russian strikes. Winter is seven weeks away.

On Iran: the US-Iran ceasefire probability sits at 92 percent through October 12 and 74 percent through October 31 on prediction markets. Iran appears to be extracting tolls on Hormuz oil traffic -- Monday's coverage described it as a potential de facto blockade charge. The US pulled its strategic bombers from RAF Fairford Monday, and on Thursday Meduza reported that the White House has ordered the Pentagon to develop Iran strike options that could be executed before the November midterms. The combination of withdrawn bomber posture and domestic political incentive for a pre-election strike creates a genuinely unstable information environment around what US Iran policy actually is.

VIP flight data shows AF2 operating over western Maryland and Pennsylvania Friday -- a domestic political trip. UK Royal Air Force (RRR9915) was tracking over the eastern Mediterranean at 32.5°N, 33°E on Friday -- near Cyprus or the Israeli coast. Netherlands (EAGLE7) was over the Ionian Sea. Multiple Belgian F-16 formations were active across Europe and over Atlantic approaches. The alliance posture is diffuse: no single concentration of NATO air activity, which is consistent with deterrence-by-presence rather than pre-strike massing.

Ethiopia launched a drone strike against Eritrean troops that had crossed into Tigray on Friday, while reporting confirmed that Tigray rebel factions are forcibly recruiting boys as young as 15 as fighting spreads. The Horn of Africa deterioration is largely absent from Western attention this week, which is consistent with a pattern in which Horn conflicts enter Western awareness only during acute humanitarian crises.

## Commentary digest

The week's commentary thread worth following runs from AI to the labor market to the political economy of fiscal adjustment.

Ingar Haaland's note via Marginal Revolution (Friday): with mathematics increasingly delegated to AI, the top tier of economics research will improve while the social return to mediocre research falls near zero. This argument generalizes: AI is compressing the middle of many knowledge-work distributions. Caixin documented the same dynamic hitting Chinese middle management this week -- firms replacing analytics and coordination layers with AI systems.

Tyler Cowen covered "The Great Accretion and the Great Depression" (Tuesday): the thesis is that the Second Industrial Revolution's productivity wave created the very financial optimism that metastasized into the 1929 crash. The parallel is obvious and Cowen does not belabor it, but the analogy implies that the current AI productivity surge may be building instabilities that will not be visible until after the fact.

Tim Taylor's Friday piece on the tax-side agenda for US budget deficits is the most policy-relevant commentary of the week. The argument: you cannot get to fiscal sustainability by cutting spending alone at current deficit levels. The 140-basis-point spread between the federal funds rate and the 10-year Treasury is doing some of the arguing for him in real time.

Gita Gopinath's Conversations with Tyler interview (running through the week as a Conversable Economist feature) on trade and currencies is worth the full read: her framework for how currency misalignment interacts with tariff policy is directly relevant to the dollar's current strength and the question of whether a strong dollar is doing the work that tariffs were supposed to do.
