## Headline of the week

The collision between Trump's self-declared energy ceasefire and the mechanics of a war that neither Russia nor Ukraine has any incentive to pause on American terms dominated the week, while Houthi attacks on shipping near Hormuz reached a record pace and pushed WTI crude oil above $100 per barrel for the first time since September. The two crises are not separate: the same Hormuz oil-market premium that is complicating Trump's domestic political calculus is also the pressure he is trying, badly, to offload onto Zelensky. The week ended with Ukraine announcing a resumption of Russian refinery strikes and with EU leaders convening in Brussels to discuss sustaining Kyiv's support structure without Washington. The regime shift is this: the US is no longer a reliable broker of either the Ukraine conflict or the Hormuz corridor, and European and energy markets are pricing that in.

## Macro: how the picture moved

The yield curve flattened further across the week. The 2-year Treasury yield rose 7 basis points (4.75% to 4.82% as of October 14), while the 10-year fell 3 basis points (5.22% to 5.19%) and the 30-year fell 8 basis points (5.60% to 5.52%). The 10-year-minus-2-year spread narrowed from 0.44% to 0.33%, a decline of 11 basis points, the sharpest weekly move in the spread in several weeks. Fed funds effective rate held at 3.88% and SOFR at 3.88%, with prediction markets pricing 86.5% probability of no change at the October 29 FOMC meeting and 13.5% probability of a 25 basis point rate increase. The probability of a 50 basis point cut at this meeting stands at effectively zero.

The simultaneous flattening and rally in long bonds (TLT closed Thursday at 79.97, +1.37%) while equities also advanced is the characteristic fingerprint of geopolitical risk-off entering a risk-on market. Investors are not fleeing equities; they are hedging via duration and real assets. Gold (GLD 382.71, +1.79% on the day, up sharply on the week) and silver (SLV 51.92, +3.39% Thursday) registered the largest single-session precious-metal moves of the quarter. WTI crude rose from $96.24 at the start of the week to $101.91 by October 14, a gain of roughly $5.67 or 5.9%, driven directly by the Hormuz shipping situation.

Read together: the curve is flattening because the short end is pricing a Fed that may need to tighten against oil-driven inflation, while the long end is pricing growth concerns and a flight to duration. The VIX rose from 14.84 Monday to 16.87 by Wednesday, a 2-point gain that is modest but directionally clear. The dollar was fractionally weaker (EUR/USD 1.1193 versus 1.1259 on the week; UUP -0.41% Thursday), consistent with some safe-haven competition from gold rather than the dollar.

Fed balance sheet stood at $6.76 trillion as of October 14. Core PCE last printed at 130.455 (August); headline CPI index at 336.298 (September). The unemployment rate held at 4.2% (September) with nonfarm payrolls at 159,044 thousand. These lagged indicators support a Fed that is watching and not moving; the oil spike is the joker that could change that calculus before year-end.

## Markets: rotation and dispersion

The week's clearest dispersion: small caps (IWM 275.08, +1.37% Thursday) outpaced both large caps (SPY 771.79, +0.28%) and growth-heavy tech (QQQ 728.00, -0.13%). This is consistent with a market rotation toward domestically oriented sectors ahead of the October 29 FOMC meeting, where any hint of a pause extension would benefit rate-sensitive small companies. The QQQ underperformance is worth watching given the AI capital expenditure cycle; if tech cools near-term while rates stay elevated, the capex assumptions in large-cap tech earnings will face pressure.

China equities (FXI 34.26, +1.18% Thursday) advanced while broader emerging markets declined (EEM 63.96, -0.67%). This divergence appeared consistently across the week's data. The Chinese internal feed noted that ICBC regained the top position in A-share market capitalization, displacing Suanmiao Technology, a domestic AI chipmaker that in the same week closed a fundraise of approximately 2 billion yuan (roughly $300 million) for first-generation production targeting 2027. These are not independent: state financial sector reasserting dominance while private AI chipmakers raise capital at scale. Both are relevant to the semiconductor export control watch area.

Credit markets tightened, not widened: HYG closed Thursday at 77.13 (+0.42%), LQD at 103.50 (+0.82%). Investment grade and high yield both rallied with long duration. That is a coherent signal for a regime where geopolitical risk is not yet being priced as a credit event. The moment Hormuz-driven oil inflation forces the Fed's hand or materially narrows margins in industrial borrowers, that changes.

Energy and commodities dominated the weekly commodity landscape. USO gained 2.44% on Thursday alone. Natural gas (UNG -1.16%) declined as European winter demand expectations eased slightly. The silver surge (+3.39%) outpaced gold (+1.79%), a ratio move that has historically preceded sustained precious-metals rallies.

## Politics and policy: what moved in Washington and elsewhere

The Trump administration's unilateral announcement of an "energy ceasefire" on October 11 was the political event of the week. Trump declared via Truth Social that Ukraine and Russia had agreed to an immediate halt to strikes on energy infrastructure. Zelensky said the announcement was news to him. Russia offered no acceptance. Within hours of the announcement, Russian strikes on Ukrainian power plants continued. Zelensky had privately offered a "refinery for refinery" reciprocal arrangement; the White House framed this as a lopsided deal given Russia's larger refinery network and Ukraine's severely limited one. By October 15, Zelensky announced that Ukraine was resuming strikes on Russian oil refineries, citing continued Russian attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure as the basis for abandoning the pause.

Trump publicly called Zelensky "more difficult to deal with than Putin" during the week and, per multiple sourced reports, urged Ukraine to select new leadership. Elon Musk publicly endorsed Trump's position. The EU response, visible in both the foreign news and Ukrainian internal feeds, was to convene a leadership meeting in Brussels on October 16 to affirm continued support for Ukraine without American participation and to advance discussions on the use of frozen Russian sovereign assets. Kaja Kallas (European Council) was quoted saying that Europe is working to persuade the US that pressure should fall on Russia, not Ukraine, and is exploring deploying frozen assets in Kyiv's favor.

On the domestic political front, California filed its 100th lawsuit against the Trump administration, this time targeting ICE courthouse arrests. Trump counter-sued California over allegations of noncitizen voter registration. Both actions are symbolic escalations in an ongoing legal war between the state and federal government that has become a structured feature of the political landscape rather than a series of discrete disputes. FEC filings in the period show Mary Peltola (D-AK) at $32.62 million raised for the 2026 Alaska Senate cycle, with Susan Collins (R-ME) at $24.13 million. Prediction markets give Democrats a 90.5% probability of controlling the House after the November 3 midterms.

Outside the US: Hungary's parliament reversed central provisions of the Orban-era 2021 "child protection law" targeting LGBTQ+ communities, the first significant legislative reversal of that era's policies under the new government. Slovakia announced the restoration of military-diplomatic representation with Russia for the first time since 2022, drawing warnings of espionage risk from NATO allies. Poland's police received information about a possible assassination threat targeting Prime Minister Tusk and Defense Minister Kosinyak-Kamysz. Turkey suspended opposition television broadcaster Halk TV on October 14, placing it under external management.

## Sanctions and legal

OFAC issued Iran-related designations on October 15, including a Cuba designation and issuance of new and amended general licenses. The Hormuz-adjacent framing of these is direct: as tanker attacks at Hormuz reach record pace, sanctions maintenance against Iran serves as a policy anchor even as the US-Iran ceasefire continues (81.5% prediction market probability through October 31).

The Nord Stream pipeline sabotage trial opened in Hamburg on October 13. A Ukrainian citizen faces charges of attacking the Nord Stream 1 and 2 pipelines in September 2022. The case raises unresolved questions about Kyiv's command responsibility in a period when Germany and Ukraine are cooperating closely against Russia, and German domestic politics around Ukraine support are already under scrutiny.

The BAE Systems cyberattack, disclosed October 16, disrupted the Administrative Office of the US Courts' notice system for federal bankruptcy courts. BAE Systems is a government contractor. This is the third major government-adjacent infrastructure compromise visible in this week's data (alongside BeyondTrust's China-linked zero-day and the earlier French supermarket refrigeration cyberattack affecting Carrefour systems).

On the judicial calendar, the Court of International Trade tariff docket and SCOTUS oral argument calendars are not directly visible in this week's courtlistener feed, which captured six Court of Appeals and state supreme court opinions. The broader court trajectory from the federal register includes a final Treasury rule clarifying consolidated group transfer liabilities and proposed Coast Guard safety zones, but no major regulatory reversals.

## Geopolitical signal

The Houthi attack on Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport on October 11 killed at least 12 people and injured over 300; the airport was closed indefinitely. By October 12, a second drone wave hit the facility. This was the highest-casualty Houthi strike on Saudi civilian infrastructure in the current campaign. Saudi Arabia vowed retaliation. Riyadh's economy, significantly dependent on aviation connectivity, absorbed a direct operational hit during a peak travel period.

BBC Verify analysis of UK maritime agency data found that 12 vessels were struck near the Strait of Hormuz in a single week, described as a record. Prediction markets give only 0.55% probability that Hormuz traffic returns to normal by October 31. The US-Iran ceasefire is holding (81.5% probability of continuation through month-end), but the physical attacks on shipping continue at an accelerating pace, suggesting the ceasefire does not extend to proxy maritime operations. The US announced an end to the Iranian blockade with 5.5% probability by October 31, indicating markets expect the de facto blockade to persist.

Ukraine struck three of six Yandex data centers on October 11 (in Sasovo/Ryazan, Kaluga, and Vladimir), described by Kyiv as a response to Russian attacks on Ukrainian data infrastructure. Yandex services experienced disruptions across Russia and in Kazakhstan, Armenia, and Serbia; full repair of damaged equipment was estimated at up to six months.

Starlink experienced a 24-hour interruption of Starshield service to Ukrainian military units on October 14, disrupting drone communications. Both the White House and Elon Musk denied responsibility. A congressional investigation has been initiated. This is a critical vulnerability in Ukraine's ground-level combat communications and the event will likely have structural consequence for how Ukraine plans contingency connectivity going forward.

Russian drones struck Rivne University on October 13. The NATO Supreme Commander presented a public "victory theory" for defending against Russian attack, naming potential strikes on Saint Petersburg and Kaliningrad in a deterrence scenario.

Russia arrested a Japanese journalist on espionage allegations on October 14. Russia also imposed visa requirements on Montenegro. EU member states discussed restricting standard Schengen visas for Russian citizens, potentially limiting travel to country-specific authorization.

## Commentary digest

The Nobel Economics Prize awarded to Robert Hall (Stanford) and Michael Woodford (Columbia) on October 12, for "microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization," landed directly in the week's central debate. Their models, which inform how central banks calibrate timing of monetary interventions during shocks, are the theoretical basis for the exact problem now facing the Fed: an oil-supply shock from geopolitical sources that is not a demand signal and that the standard stabilization toolkit cannot cleanly address. The Conversable Economist covered the prize with this framing explicitly. The committee chair, John Hassler, cited energy crises as a prime use case for the laureates' contribution.

The Conversable Economist also published a sharp piece on the incoherence of Trump's tariff announcements, citing research from the Global Linkages Lab at Brown University (Kalemli-Ozcan, Soylu, and Yildirim). The piece noted internally contradictory signals in successive administration trade announcements that make planning by firms and trading partners structurally impossible. Combined with the California litigation escalation and the mid-cycle FEC fundraising data, this suggests a domestic political economy in which uncertainty is itself the regime, not a temporary condition.

Marginal Revolution published an exchange between Tyler Cowen and Dwarkesh Patel, recorded for the NYT podcast, on unreleased AI models and what they signal about internal capabilities gaps between publicly and privately known frontier performance. In the same week, CERT-In (India) warned that AI agents under testing reached live production systems of Indian organizations and read confidential business records. These two data points, from different angles, converge on the same risk: the frontier of deployed AI capability is ahead of the governance frameworks designed to contain it.

Alex Tabarrok's piece on India's labor law rigidity, comparing it unfavorably to Germany, connects to the broader EM capital rotation story visible in markets data: EEM underperforming, FXI outperforming, and the structural question of which emerging markets can actually absorb the supply-chain relocation that US-China decoupling demands.

## Cross-section themes

**1. The Trump ceasefire regime has a half-life of hours.** Three times now, Trump has announced a Ukraine-Russia energy or infrastructure truce (September 14, October 11, and a preliminary ceasefire earlier). Each announcement collapses within 24 hours of Russian non-acceptance. The pattern is the message: Trump's mediation generates headlines and domestic political cover without producing verifiable compliance from Moscow. This is not diplomacy failing at the edges; it is a structural feature of a negotiating posture that Russia has learned to exploit.

**2. Iran is in five feeds simultaneously.** OFAC sanctions (Oct 15), Hormuz shipping attacks at record pace, the Houthi strike on Riyadh airport, prediction markets on US-Iran ceasefire and blockade, and Russian internal commentary on Iran's energy infrastructure. The US-Iran ceasefire holds by market probability but the physical infrastructure of the Hormuz chokepoint is being degraded by proxy operations that the ceasefire language does not appear to cover. WTI above $100 is the transmission mechanism from Hormuz to every other section of this brief.

**3. Government infrastructure is the attack surface.** Three separate compromises in one week: BAE Systems (federal bankruptcy courts), BeyondTrust zero-day exploited by Chinese threat actor Armory Castle since August, and the Starlink interruption to Ukrainian military units of unclear attribution. Add the Yandex data center strikes and the French refrigeration hack, and the week's pattern is consistent: the cyberattack surface has expanded to include logistics, judicial administration, and battlefield communications as primary targets, not secondary ones.

**4. The same statute, IEEPA, is driving three simultaneous legal conflicts.** OFAC Iran designations, the federal court filing activity around tariff dockets, and the California-federal litigation over ICE courthouse enforcement all route through executive authority delegated under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and related post-9/11 frameworks. The volume of IEEPA-adjacent legal activity in a single week suggests this statute is becoming the primary instrument of executive unilateralism across trade, immigration, and foreign policy simultaneously.

**5. The yield curve as geopolitical thermometer.** The 10y-2y spread fell 11 basis points this week, not because growth expectations collapsed, but because the 2-year is pricing possible Fed tightening against oil inflation while the 10-year is pricing geopolitical flight-to-safety. Gold, silver, and long bonds rallied together while VIX nudged up. This is not a recession signal; it is a stagflation hedge building in real time, consistent with an oil supply shock from an irreducible geopolitical source (Hormuz) that the Fed cannot address with conventional tools.
