# Weekly brief -- 2026-W32 (August 2 through August 7)

> **Note:** Weekly bundle for 2026-W32 was not yet generated; this brief is composed from five consecutive daily bundles (Aug 2-7) plus web verification.

## Headline of the week

The Strait of Hormuz dominated every signal in the system this week and its closure touched every other dataset in the feeds. A deal brokered through Oman appeared to be "in final stages" as of Friday but had not been signed -- and the market whipsawing around each rumor was the most visible signature of how completely one geopolitical variable is now driving global asset prices. Running alongside the Hormuz thread: Russia's revived Black Sea blockade squeezing Ukraine's export economy while Ukrainian strikes pushed Russian refinery throughput to its lowest since 2002; Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan formalizing a mutual defense architecture -- the "Mecca Joint Defence Agreement" -- that nuclear-armed Pakistan had previously been careful to avoid; and a US Federal Reserve that is now more likely to hike in September than cut, driven by an oil shock nobody had priced four months ago.

## Macro: how the picture moved

Without a weekly macro_recap file, this section is reconstructed from daily FRED series across the five trading sessions.

Treasury yields declined across the curve during the week, but the move was modest and the narrative was complicated. The 10-year went from approximately 4.75% (July 31 read, carried into the Monday bundle) to 4.63% by Thursday's close -- a 12 basis point drop -- and the 2-year fell in parallel from 4.28% to 4.18%. That is a rally in duration. But the 10-2 spread actually tightened fractionally (0.47 to 0.44), and gold surged nearly 5% on the week (GLD: $371 to $389). These two things together -- yields down, gold up, curve flatter -- point to a market pricing stagflationary risk rather than a clean safe-haven or growth-scare bid. The bond market is not rallying in celebration; it is rallying because growth fears are competing with inflation fears and neither is winning.

The more consequential macro story is on the short end. The Fed held rates at 3.50-3.75% in July (9-3 vote; three members dissented in favor of an immediate hike). That dissent, combined with oil staying elevated because the Hormuz blockade was not lifted, has repriced the September meeting decisively. JPMorgan is now penciling in a 25 basis-point hike. Futures markets are pricing roughly 65% probability of a September increase. That is a complete flip from cut expectations earlier in the year, and it matters structurally: if the Fed hikes into an Iranian oil shock, the US risks a 1970s-style adverse supply shock compounded by tighter monetary policy precisely when tariff-tax revenues have already slowed. Robert Pape (University of Chicago) described Trump's Iran position this week as an "escalation trap." That trap has a monetary-policy corollary -- the Fed cannot cut while oil is elevated by a conflict it cannot control, but hiking risks tipping an already tariff-stressed economy into contraction.

WTI crude (proxied by USO) was the most volatile asset of the week: opening near $129 equivalent on Monday, collapsing 5.2% on Wednesday when Oman deal rumors pushed the "blockade ends by Aug 7" prediction-market contract to 45%, then recovering 3.5% Thursday-Friday when no deal was announced. The Hormuz deal's non-closure on the Aug 7 deadline landed that contract back at 12-15%. The spread between USO's implied crude and spot WTI is wide because tanker insurance rates and routing-risk premiums remain embedded in delivered prices -- even a reopening announcement would not immediately normalize insurance markets.

The dollar (UUP) ended the week essentially flat (+0.03%), the yen strengthened modestly (FXY implied JPY/USD around 159, firmer than the 163 handle of late July), and EUR/USD held around 1.1519. European equities (EFA) barely moved, underperforming EM (EEM +1.5%) and the US (SPY +2.9% for the week before a Friday pullback). EM outperformance reflects the options value embedded in an Oman deal: lower oil would disproportionately benefit current-account-deficit EM economies. Europe is trapped: elevated gas prices with no oil-shock upside and no Hormuz exposure relief.

## Markets: rotation and dispersion

The week's signature move was the mid-week technology rally. QQQ gained approximately 3.40% on Wednesday (Aug 5) before Friday gave back some gains. The Russell 2000 (IWM) outperformed large caps through Thursday but drew down more on Friday (-0.51% vs. -0.16% for SPY), consistent with small-cap sensitivity to September hike risk -- domestic rate-sensitive companies cannot absorb a tightening cycle the way mega-caps can.

The week's most informative spread was gold versus oil. Gold gained ~4.9% (GLD: $371 to $389, implying spot near $3,150 -- near all-time highs) while USO lost approximately 8% net before partial recovery. In a standard risk-off week, both rise together; when gold rises while oil falls or whipsaws, the market is pricing geopolitical risk repricing toward slow resolution, not imminent escalation. But gold at all-time highs is also consistent with a market pricing the Fed-into-stagflation scenario described above.

Credit markets showed no stress (HYG nearly flat, LQD -0.36% on week), and VIX futures (VXX) fell from 21.25 to 20.23. The disconnect between declining volatility and surging gold is worth tracking: options-market participants are less concerned about tail risk than gold-market participants. That divergence has historically resolved toward the gold market being right.

Two utility-sector developments merit mention. NextEra Energy and Dominion Energy filed for regulatory approval of their $67 billion merger with FERC, the NRC, and multiple state commissions in mid-July; Form 425 filings appeared in the SEC feed this week. The combined company would be the world's largest regulated utility, serving roughly 10 million customers in the southeastern US. Simultaneously, the Trump administration paid $1.2 billion to Germany's RWE to cancel offshore wind projects -- the latest in a string of buyouts that are effectively reshaping the US renewable energy footprint. These two developments point in opposite directions: one bet on electrification demand growth (NextEra-Dominion), one dismantling the generation capacity to meet that demand.

## Politics and policy: what moved in Washington and elsewhere

Three executive actions defined domestic political activity this week.

First, Trump signed two new birthright citizenship executive orders targeting "birth tourism" and children of foreign government employees and terrorist organization members -- less than two months after the Supreme Court struck down his original 2025 citizenship order 6-3 in June. The new orders are narrower and more tailored to survive constitutional scrutiny; litigation is already underway, and the orders will generate months of circuit court activity before any SCOTUS revisit.

Second, the administration suffered its 21st consecutive court defeat in its campaign to obtain state voter rolls. Courts have uniformly held that states are not required to surrender voter registration records to the federal government. The pattern -- 21 straight losses across multiple circuits -- is establishing a pre-existing record of systematic challenge that could be deployed rhetorically to contest Republican losses in the November 3 midterms regardless of the underlying litigation outcome.

Third, the administration refunded approximately $100 billion in "Liberation Day" tariff revenue to affected businesses (roughly 60% of total collections), while simultaneously imposing a new 15% polysilicon tariff under Section 232. The tariff refund reduces near-term fiscal drag from trade policy and functions as pre-midterm business relations; the polysilicon action escalates on the China technology front. Trade policy is being unwound and tightened simultaneously in different sectors.

FEC fundraising data confirms Democrats' financial dominance in major Senate contests: Ossoff (GA) at $97.9M, Talarico (TX) at $68.6M, Brown (OH) at $38.6M, Cooper (NC) at $34.9M, and Booker (NJ) at $33.5M -- all with strong net cash positions. Speaker Johnson (LA) leads Republicans at $21M. Democrats are outraising Republicans at roughly a 4-to-1 ratio in the marquee Senate races with fewer than 90 days to Election Day.

## Sanctions and legal

The sanctions feed remained heavily weighted toward Russian financial institution designations: Belgazprombank, MIR Business Bank, Sberbank Europe, Novikombank, Vitabank, and multiple Russian regional banks appeared across all five daily feeds, all carrying multi-source designation (OFAC, EU FSF, UK FCDO, Canada, Switzerland, Australia). The consistency across daily feeds suggests these are stable designations being carried forward rather than a burst of new actions -- though the weekly accumulation provides an accurate picture of where the G7+ sanctions perimeter currently stands on Russian bank exposure.

Two genuinely new signals surfaced. The UK announced additional Russia sanctions targeting shadow fleet vessels and banks this week (appearing in the GKG feed); Sweden confirmed the seizure and transfer to Ukraine of a Russian shadow fleet ship -- a legal precedent that could accelerate European interdiction of the approximately 600-vessel shadow fleet. Each shadow fleet seizure is a data point toward the eventual question of whether the EU can systematically interdict the fleet that is keeping Russian oil revenue flowing.

ALCHEMICAL SOLUTIONS PRIVATE LIMITED, an Indian entity, appeared in the OFAC SDN database in the sanctions feed. This is significant because India appears repeatedly in the US-Russia trade watch data (South Asian intermediaries routing goods into the Russian economy), and OFAC is using the SDN list to pressure third-country intermediaries in a way that creates compliance risk for Indian firms broadly -- not just this company.

CourtListener showed the 7th Circuit deciding the Rishi Shah and Shradha Agarwal Outcome Health cases (healthcare fraud prosecutions), the Delaware Supreme Court listing Fox News v. Newsom (defamation jurisdiction), and the ongoing Trump voter-roll litigation pattern spanning multiple circuits.

## Geopolitical signal

The dominant geopolitical event of the week was the signing of the "Mecca Joint Defence Agreement" between Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan on August 7. This is qualitatively different from previous regional alignments. Turkey is a NATO member, which creates institutional overlap. Pakistan is nuclear-armed, which means Saudi Arabia is effectively extending a deterrence umbrella through association. The deal followed nearly a year of negotiations and was precipitated by Iranian missile fire on Gulf states and Houthi attacks on Saudi territory -- all traceable to the February 28 US-Israel strike on Iran that triggered the current conflict. Turkish officials described the pact as "purely defensive" and "not against any specific actor." Neither of those framings is persuasive given the context, but they are the language required to maintain Turkey's NATO membership and Pakistan's non-aligned posture.

Prediction markets kept "US invades Iran before 2027" at 18-22% throughout the week with no clear directional trend. The Hormuz probability structure is more useful: "normal by Aug 15" at 1-3%, "by Aug 31" at 12%, "by Dec 31" at 58%. Markets are pricing a slow, contested reopening -- not an immediate deal, not permanent blockade. The Iranian regime collapse question sat at 6%. Markets are not pricing a revolutionary outcome; they are pricing a government that is negotiating. The Oman channel is working because Tehran's government is still in the room.

Ukraine's Black Sea situation deteriorated sharply during the week. Russia's port-strike campaign, intensifying since early July, has frozen roughly 90% of commercial shipping at Odesa-region ports. Ukrainian authorities recorded 67 strikes on seaport facilities and 57 on vessels through August 3. The $27 billion in Ukrainian annual exports at risk -- primarily agricultural -- is the economic weapon Russia is deploying as a counterpart to Ukraine's drone offensive on Russian infrastructure. July was, per Lloyd's List, the deadliest month for merchant seamen in the Black Sea since the full invasion began in 2022.

Ukraine is not without counters. The Slavneft-Yanos refinery in Yaroslavl (one of Russia's five largest, 15 million tons annual capacity) sustained a large-scale fire from Ukrainian drone strikes on August 6. Russian refineries processed approximately 3.6 million barrels per day in July -- their lowest level since May 2002 and roughly one-third below seasonal norm. Russia is compensating by importing fuel from South Korea: nearly 30,000 tons shipped to the Russian Far East in late July from Korean refiners who benefit from Russia's diesel export ban creating global supply gaps.

The Leipzig airport incident (August 4-5) fits the Russian hybrid warfare pattern precisely. An explosives-laden drone was found on the tarmac adjacent to an Antonov AN-124 loaded with weapons for Ukraine; a DHL cargo aircraft struck an unidentified object six kilometers away, landing safely at Hanover with nose damage. German federal prosecutors took jurisdiction. BBC reporting cited previous cases of Russian-linked proxies delivering drones and explosives to Germany -- this is not an isolated incident but part of a campaign targeting the European logistics chain supplying Ukraine.

Taiwan's annualized 14% GDP growth in H1 2026 (flagged in the Marginal Revolution commentary feed) is the headline nobody is writing large enough. TSMC's dominance in advanced AI-inference and training chips, combined with global compute demand, is producing the kind of fiscal revenues and reserve accumulation that give Taiwan more economic leverage than at any prior point in cross-strait history. The week also saw Taiwan's president conducting an emergency nighttime evacuation drill -- military preparation visible to Beijing.

## Cross-section themes (the synthesis layer)

**Theme 1: The Hormuz-rate linkage, and why one variable is running everything.** Every significant macro movement this week -- the September hike repricing from zero probability to 65%, oil volatility, gold at all-time highs, and the formation of the Saudi-Turkey-Pakistan defense pact -- traces back to the Strait of Hormuz blockade. The Fed is in a policy trap: an oil shock it cannot control (caused by a war it is directly prosecuting) is forcing it toward tightening into a tariff-stressed economy. If the Oman deal closes: oil crashes, hike odds collapse, equities rally 3-5%, EM outperforms. If it drags through September: the FOMC hikes, the dollar strengthens, and the long end of the curve steepens as the market prices an adverse supply shock response. The Hormuz outcome is now the most important input to September FOMC pricing -- more important than August CPI, more important than payrolls.

**Theme 2: The dual Black Sea attrition.** Russia is strangling Ukraine's export economy from the sea while Ukraine's drone campaign is strangling Russia's refinery throughput from the sky. Both campaigns are working simultaneously. Ukraine cannot sustain $27 billion per year in frozen exports; Russia cannot sustain oil processing at 2002 levels. Which domestic economy fractures first under the attrition is the central question of the European theater. Russia importing South Korean fuel -- using a sanctions carve-out that the US has not closed -- illustrates how porous the Russia sanctions perimeter remains on energy inputs even as it tightens on financial flows.

**Theme 3: Technology supply chain sovereignty enters the materials phase.** The polysilicon tariff (Section 232), Taiwan's 14% GDP boom, ongoing semiconductor export controls, and China's first robot school all point to the same structural pivot: the US-China technology rivalry is moving from the device and design layer (chips, fab capacity) to the materials and feedstock layer (polysilicon, rare earths, process chemicals). China controls 90%+ of global polysilicon production. The December 4 effective date gives the market four months to adjust; First Solar (FSLR) -- which produces domestically -- benefits from the price floor. Downstream solar assembly and semiconductor packaging companies with Asian supply chains face margin compression.

**Theme 4: The Sunni nuclear umbrella.** The Mecca Defence Agreement places nuclear-armed Pakistan in a formal defensive alliance with Saudi Arabia. This is the most significant formal security architecture shift in the Gulf since 1979. Iranian leadership now faces the prospect that strikes on Saudi territory could trigger a response from a state with which it shares a long, contested border and which has a declared second-strike capability. This may paradoxically facilitate the Hormuz deal: by raising Iran's escalation costs, the Mecca agreement creates more room for Tehran to accept a face-saving exit through the Oman channel. Watch whether Iran's negotiating posture softens after the pact's announcement.

**Theme 5: The pre-midterm legal mobilization.** Twenty-one consecutive court losses on voter roll demands, a second round of birthright citizenship executive orders after a 6-3 SCOTUS defeat, and the $100 billion tariff refund together sketch a coherent pre-election posture: build a record of executive action (even if it loses), return money to businesses, and lay legal groundwork to dispute results. Democrats' 4-to-1 fundraising advantage in Senate races makes the midterm stakes acute. The administration's consistent courtroom losses on voter roll access may function less as legal strategy and more as political narrative -- establishing the framework for challenging results before votes are cast.

## What to watch -- next 14 days

**Hormuz deal window (August 8-15).** The Oman-mediated framework was described as "in final stages" on August 7. If signed: expect an immediate WTI crude decline of 10-15%, September hike probability collapsing from 65% to perhaps 25-30%, and a risk-on equity rally with EM outperforming. If it collapses: watch for Iran's parliament to advance legislation formally barring US/Israeli vessels, which would harden the crisis into a legal-political structure more difficult to negotiate away.

Chair Powell's post-meeting press conference will be watched for whether "transitory" has been replaced with "supply-driven" -- and whether the Fed is willing to validate the hike with a clear policy signal.

**Polysilicon tariff effective date (December 4).** Solar panel and semiconductor supply chains will begin to adjust now. Watch the spread between First Solar (FSLR, domestic beneficiary) and Asian-sourced module assemblers for early read on margin impact. Also watch whether China retaliates with rare earth export restrictions, which would escalate the materials-layer technology confrontation.

**US midterm elections (November 3).** Fewer than 90 days out. Democrats' cash advantages in GA, TX, OH, NC, and NJ are significant; the electoral environment is shaped by Iran war fatigue, tariff-driven inflation, and the perception of executive overreach. Prediction markets are currently pricing Senate outcomes as genuinely competitive despite Democratic fundraising leads.

**NextEra/Dominion Energy regulatory process.** FERC, NRC, and state commission reviews are now parallel. The deal's first major regulatory milestone -- a FERC initial decision -- is expected before year-end. The outcome will signal whether consolidated regulated utility scale can coexist with Trump's fossil-fuel preference and anti-wind posture.
