## Headline of the week

Three things happened simultaneously this week that only make sense together. While the first-ever Trump-Putin-Xi trilateral summit convened Thursday on the sidelines of APEC in Shenzhen, Ukraine destroyed the best part of 270 meters of the Crimean Bridge's road arch, sending it into the Kerch Strait. The bridge attack had been 36 hours in progress when the three leaders shook hands. Concurrently, Anthropic priced its Nasdaq IPO at $83 per share and $1.5 trillion in market value, closing on November 19 as the largest artificial-intelligence company listing on record, breaking SpaceX's mark by five months. The week thus contained a potential diplomatic reordering of the post-Cold War world, the most consequential single Ukrainian strike of the war, and the moment that agentic AI formally acquired a public cost of capital. These are not independent events.

## Macro: how the picture moved

The dominant fact in the financial data this week is a prediction-market reading that is unusual enough to require a plain statement before anything else: Polymarket assigns a 92% probability that the Federal Reserve raises rates by 25 basis points at the December 9 FOMC meeting. The probability of a cut is negligible. The Fed funds effective rate closed Wednesday at 3.88%. The 10-year Treasury yield closed at 5.07%. The 30-year is at 5.41%. The 10y-2y spread is +28 basis points, barely positive after a long inversion. The Fed is on a hiking path, not a cutting path, and markets have fully priced that in.

This sets the entire macro context. The ECB published its November 2026 Financial Stability Review on November 18, with the headline: "Risks from AI valuations, fiscal imbalances and geoeconomic tensions weigh on financial stability outlook." That formulation is striking. The ECB is flagging AI valuations specifically as a systemic risk, in the same sentence as fiscal imbalances and geopolitics. The Anthropic IPO landed the following day.

The dollar is under moderate pressure. EUR/USD is at 1.1034; JPY/USD is at 159.74, well into territory that historically triggers BoJ anxiety. CNY/USD at 6.69 is essentially stable. The yen's weakness combined with US yields pushing past 5% is a compression trade on Japanese government bond holdings and may be a reason Nikkei and other Asian equities underperformed more than US markets this week.

Fed Vice Chair Barr gave a speech Monday on AI and financial stability implications. Fed Chair Warsh gave remarks at a Productivity and Jobs Task Force conference that same day. The Kalshi market on US productivity growth over five years is currently pricing 2.2%, which Cowen calls too low. UK Q3 productivity came in at +2.5% year-over-year. These data points collectively suggest the central question for 2027 rate policy is whether AI-driven productivity offsets tariff-driven inflation. The December hike suggests the Fed does not currently believe it does.

## Markets: rotation and dispersion

SPY closed Friday at 715.87, down from Monday's open near 754.37, a week-over-week decline of approximately 5.1%. QQQ fell from 679.89 to 645.04, also roughly 5.1%. IWM fell at the same pace. This is not a rotation -- everything went down together.

The only positive week-over-week performer in the standard watchlist was TLT, up approximately 1.2% (79.38 to 80.35). Long bonds catching a bid while equities sell off is a classic flight-to-quality signature, but the magnitude is small. The 10-year yield at 5.07% suggests bonds are catching flows, not signaling a change in rate expectations. Gold (GLD) fell sharply from 355.45 Monday to 335.18 Friday, a 5.7% decline over the week, with the heaviest selling on Thursday (-3.2%) and Friday (-1.76%). Gold falling alongside equities is unusual. The most plausible read is a forced-liquidation dynamic or a reversal of the gold-as-inflation-hedge trade as the Fed's December hike became fully priced.

WTI crude (USO) peaked Monday at 145.58 and fell to 136.11 by Thursday before recovering to 138.09 Friday (+1.45%). The recovery on Friday is notable: oil was bid on a day when equities were down nearly 2%, possibly on the Crimean Bridge attack and Middle East risk premium, possibly on supply-side data. Adam Tooze's Wednesday newsletter flagged "oil markets in glut" despite prices in the high 80s and low 90s per barrel (WTI) -- the FRED series DCoilWTICO shows $89.53 as of November 17. The forward curve is pricing in structural oversupply even as spot prices hold elevated. Natural gas fell 3.3% Thursday.

Sector rotation is not visible in the aggregate data. The dispersion story this week is tech specifically. The Nvidia leadership crisis (Jensen Huang resigned November 12; board refused reinstatement at earnings call November 18; Colette Kress serving as interim CEO) and the Anthropic IPO created concentrated uncertainty in large-cap AI. Chinese tech was separately hit: FXI (China large-cap ETF) fell 1.85% Thursday. Caixin reported that China's fixed-asset investment decline deepened, industrial output growth slowed, and retail sales growth came in at only 0.4%, well below forecasts.

## Geopolitical signal

**Shenzhen trilateral.** The Trump-Putin-Xi meeting on the APEC sidelines on November 19 lasted under two hours in plenary format; Trump and Putin held a bilateral that sources describe as running approximately 40 minutes. There was no pre-agreed agenda, which the Financial Times called "unusual." The trilateral format itself -- never attempted by these three leaders in this configuration -- is the signal, not any reported outcome. Russia put what sources describe as maximalist terms on the table in the broader Abu Dhabi-initiated Ukraine peace process (November 10-11), including demands about NATO membership for Moldova and Finland in addition to Ukrainian territorial questions. That those maximalist terms preceded the summit matters: Putin went to Shenzhen with known asks and no softening signal.

Concurrently, Russian diesel tankers dispatched to US ports under a reported Trump-Putin fuel deal arrived in Brazilian ports instead, with none entering American harbors as of November 20. This is a minor but concrete piece of counter-evidence to the proposition that the US-Russia relationship is normalizing commercially.

**Crimean Bridge.** Ukraine attacked the Kerch Bridge on November 16 and 17 in what analysts are calling an unprecedented operation. A 270-meter section of the road arch collapsed into the strait. Traffic was suspended. Rail traffic on the parallel arch appears to have continued, though it was separately suspended after follow-on attacks. Ukraine's General Staff confirmed the attack used Flamingo missiles and explicitly stated the operation did not use Starlink or Starshield -- a specific assertion of operational independence from US satellite infrastructure at a moment when that independence has political significance given the Musk-Starlink-Ukraine context flagged in earlier US Senate correspondence. Russia retaliated with strikes on Kyiv bridges on the night of November 16-17 and again November 19-20, killing three people in the later attack. Ukrainian drones meanwhile hit oil storage in Tatarstan (near Almetyevsk), oil refineries in Krasnodar region, and an explosives-component chemical plant in Smolensk oblast.

Japan scrambled jets November 17 after suspected Russian aircraft violated Japanese airspace. The incident coincides with the APEC summit period and may have been a signal or a miscalculation.

The Israel-Iran ceasefire is holding: Polymarket assigns 94-99% probability it continues through November 22. US military transport aircraft (RCH callsigns) were visible in the Eastern Mediterranean as of November 20 flight data.

## Cross-section themes (the synthesis layer)

**1. AI's cost of capital is arriving.** The Anthropic IPO, the Nvidia succession crisis, the ECB's explicit flagging of AI valuations as a systemic risk, California's AI safety kill-switch framework, the Fed Reserve's Barr speech on AI and financial stability, and the AIs-vs-economists-papers study all landed in the same week. The transition from AI-as-research-project to AI-as-regulated-financial-entity is structurally underway. Cowen's reading of the IPO discount as bearish on extreme superintelligence is worth holding: the market priced $1.5 trillion, not $2 trillion, in a rising-rate environment with equities falling 5%. Real capital is now making real bets, with real hurdle rates.

**2. The Crimean Bridge and Starlink independence.** Ukraine's operational confirmation that the November 17 strike required no Starlink dependency is significant in context. US Senate Democrats had earlier sent Musk preservation-of-documents letters over Starlink restrictions. Ukraine's answer is that it can conduct major infrastructure strikes without it. This changes the leverage calculus in any Trump-mediated negotiation that uses Starlink access as a chip.

**3. The trilateral geometry.** Canada-China tariff reduction (November 17), the APEC Trump-Putin-Xi meeting (November 19), Caixin reporting on China's AI infrastructure constraints post-Nvidia, and China's fixed-asset investment decline all describe a world in which the United States is not the organizing pole for major economies. Canada is diversifying. China is managing headwinds. Russia is seeking recognition. The trilateral format itself normalizes direct great-power negotiation over regional rules; the week's absence of a joint statement is consistent with process-without-resolution, which is structurally different from impasse.

**4. The Fed and the fiscal arithmetic.** A 92% probability of a December hike with the 10-year at 5.07% and the 30-year at 5.41% puts the federal interest expense trajectory on a steeper path than the ECB's fiscal imbalances warning acknowledged. Bitcoin's probability of dipping to $60,000 by year-end sits at 82% on Polymarket, suggesting crypto is following the rates repricing rather than acting as an inflation hedge. Gold's 5.7% weekly decline is consistent with the same read.

**5. Civil service and the administrative state.** Three simultaneous OPM direct-final-rule confirmations, the HAVANA Act DHS implementation, and the ongoing Federal Register pattern of agency rule-rewriting describes an administrative acceleration that is below media salience but systematically changing the operational rules of federal employment. The civil service restructuring is happening in the Federal Register at a pace that daily coverage does not capture; the weekly view makes it visible.
