## Headline of the week

Two central bank decisions in four days crystallized the transatlantic monetary regime without resolving it. The Fed held its target range at 3.75-4.00% on Wednesday in a 10-2 vote -- with dissenters presumed to want a hike now rather than waiting -- while markets kept the 2-year Treasury at 4.67-4.70%, a spread of roughly 80 basis points above the effective fed funds rate of 3.88%. The ECB matched the Fed by holding at 2.5% on Thursday, then immediately announced Christine Lagarde's departure for February 7, 2027, opening a succession question inside a tightening credit environment. On the same Friday that Lagarde's exit date became public, Alphabet's earnings confirmed what AI skeptics had long feared: its own AI Overviews are cannibalizing search ad revenue, erasing roughly $500 billion in market capitalization and sending the Nasdaq 100 down 1.42%. And with US midterm elections five days out, Polymarket priced a Democratic House at 94% and a full Democratic sweep of both chambers at 70%, while the largest Senate fundraising haul in this cycle -- James Talarico's $146 million Texas Senate campaign -- signaled how capital is already positioning for legislative change.

## Macro: how the picture moved

The rates picture moved from moderately elevated to elevated-and-steepening across the week. The 10-year Treasury rose from 4.96% on Monday to 5.05% by Wednesday, while the 2-year held in a narrow band of 4.65-4.70%. The result is a 10y-2y spread that widened from 0.31 to 0.37-0.38 -- a bear steepening, where longer-dated rates are rising faster than shorter-dated ones. This is not a benign normalization of a previously inverted curve; it is term-premium expansion. Fiscal concerns, a December hike being priced, and possible foreign selling of Treasuries are the plausible drivers. At 5.05% on the 10-year and 5.37% on the 30-year, mortgage and commercial real estate debt service costs sit at stress levels that will continue to slow credit-sensitive parts of the economy even without another Fed move.

The FOMC held on a 10-2 vote -- the first dissents in months, and presumably from hawks who wanted to hike immediately. The Fed funds effective rate sits at 3.88%, comfortably inside the 3.75-4.00% target band. What the 10-year at 5.05% tells you is that markets do not believe the current rate level will persist; they are pricing a path toward 4.25-4.50% by mid-2027. That is a meaningful tightening signal even if the Fed fires no additional rounds before then.

The ECB's simultaneous hold at 2.5% ran against a pre-meeting probability of a hike to 2.75% that stood near 60% (from a September survey). The decision to pause likely reflects the bank lending survey -- published Monday, showing tightened credit conditions across the eurozone -- which gave policymakers reason to wait. Lagarde's departure announcement, confirmed February 7, 2027 rather than the original October 2027 end of term, accelerates the succession clock. Her successor will inherit a 2.5% policy rate, a tightening lending environment, and an unresolved debate about whether the ECB should be hiking into slowing credit or pausing to observe the transmission lag. That is a genuine regime inflection, and the currency market absorbed it with surprising composure: EUR/USD held near 1.12 through the week.

JPY/USD at 156.87 remains at levels historically associated with Bank of Japan intervention concern. CNY/USD near 6.67 reflects continued dollar firmness against the renminbi.

## Markets: rotation and dispersion

The week produced a textbook rotation: large-cap technology underperformed, small-caps led, and the divergence widened sharply on Friday. SPY gained roughly 1.2% on the week (759.97 to 769.36), reaching a mid-week high near 772 before Friday's reversal. QQQ (Nasdaq 100) gained about 1.5% across the week but fell 1.42% on Friday, essentially retracing the Google premium. IWM (Russell 2000) gained 3.0% for the week and added 0.97% on Friday alone -- the only major US equity index in positive territory on a day when QQQ fell sharply. This is rotation, not incidental noise.

The Friday narrative is clean: Alphabet's third-quarter earnings, released after the close on October 28, revealed that AI Overviews are eating search ad revenue. The market read this not as "AI is failing" but as "Google has built an internal cost center that cannibalizes its own most profitable business, with no offsetting monetization visible yet." The beneficiaries were everything away from mega-cap growth: small-caps, value, international developed markets (EFA +0.65% on Friday, EAFE outperforming QQQ every day this week), and Treasury bonds (TLT +0.62% on Friday as capital rotated out of tech).

Oil (USO) was the week's volatility story. The crude proxy surged 4.32% in the Sunday-Monday window -- likely reflecting Middle East supply concern ahead of the October 31 US-Iran ceasefire resolution date -- then gave back those gains through Wednesday (falling to 163.88, -1.41%), then recovered to 168.19 by Friday. The net weekly move was essentially flat, but the intraweek range of roughly $5 in underlying equivalent was significant. Polymarket at 93% for ceasefire continuation through October 31 tells you the market sees this as a near-certain hold; the oil volatility cluster suggests some fraction of players is still paying to hedge a small tail risk.

Gold (GLD) gained 0.5% on the week but sold off 1.32% on Friday. Silver (SLV) fell 3.63% on Friday, a larger beta move than gold. Natural gas (UNG) fell 3.37% on Friday. The Friday commodity selloff, in an environment where the dollar was essentially flat (UUP -0.07%), suggests unwinding of inflation hedges as the Fed's pause signals that disinflation remains in play even as the long end rises.

VXX (VIX futures) declined all week, from 18.72 on Monday to 17.41 on Friday. Complacency entering a midterm election with active geopolitical risk is itself a signal: the market's base case is an uneventful election and a continued Iran ceasefire.

## Politics and policy: what moved in Washington and elsewhere

The dominant Washington story is the midterm election, now five days out. With prediction markets at 94% for Democrats to take the House and 70% for a full sweep of both chambers, the question is no longer who wins but what follows. The fundraising data underscores the asymmetry: Democratic Senate candidates are out-raising Republican counterparts by factors of 2-4 in competitive states. James Talarico (D-TX) has raised $146 million, the largest Texas Senate haul in history and competitive with top-tier national Senate races. Jon Ossoff (D-GA) has raised $136 million. Roy Cooper (D-NC) is at $49.6 million with the highest net cash reserve of the group relative to disbursements ($9.1 million net). AOC, running for a New York House seat, has raised $38 million with $13.8 million in net cash -- an unusual war chest that suggests either an anticipated competitive race or positioning for a future statewide campaign. Sherrod Brown's Ohio Senate race at $68.4 million raised suggests the Republicans may not be able to hold Ohio either.

Executive action this week was concentrated in trade. Presidential Document 11076 (signed October 23, published October 30) further modifies the scope of Canadian motor vehicle tariffs under the framework established to offset Canadian discrimination against US auto commerce. The escalatory ladder on Canada has been climbing since early 2025; this document narrows the carve-outs, making the tariff perimeter tighter. On October 28, the White House also adjusted imports of wind turbines and their components -- a notable extension of tariff reach into clean energy supply chains -- and issued a DPA Section 303 determination on defense industrial base priorities.

The UK parliament debate on defence spending, visible in the international news feed all week, reflects a parallel structural dynamic: European governments are managing the political cost of committing to higher defence spending on explicit timetables. The UK government's current posture -- acknowledging the pressure without specifying a figure -- is consistent with the broader NATO trajectory but slower than the pace France and Poland have adopted.

## Sanctions and legal

OFAC had an unusually busy week, and the sequencing matters. On October 26, OFAC amended its FAQ on blocking and rejecting transactions under existing sanctions programs -- a technical update to compliance mechanics that practitioners watch closely. On October 27, Extreme Networks settled for apparent sanctions violations involving what is almost certainly sale of networking equipment into a sanctioned jurisdiction through third-country intermediaries; the IT infrastructure sector has been a persistent target. On October 28, simultaneous designations under both the North Korea and Democratic Republic of the Congo programs -- the DRC designation is the less expected of the two and connects to the conflict minerals and armed group financing docket (coltan, gold) in eastern DRC, a region that intersects with the critical minerals watch area.

The headline item, however, is General License FF-1 under the Iranian Transactions and Sanctions Regulations, published October 29 -- the day before the US-Iran ceasefire's October 31 resolution window. The license authorizes certain specific transactions within the Iranian sanctions perimeter. The timing is not coincidental: OFAC is constructing the financial architecture that accompanies a ceasefire framework, licensing the economic channels that the parties need to function. Whether additional licenses follow in November will indicate whether this is a tactical accommodation for a short-term pause or the scaffolding for a longer negotiated framework.

At the Court of International Trade, Jinko Solar v. United States (October 29) continues the persistent solar panel tariff litigation docket, now in its fourth year. The 9th Circuit heard Colvin and Son v. Burgum and Uber Technologies v. City of Seattle, the latter having implications for gig economy regulatory preemption. Greater Boston Legal Services v. U.S. Department of Homeland Security (1st Circuit) continues the immigration enforcement challenge cluster.

## Geopolitical signal

The Israeli election is the week's largest geopolitical discontinuity. Israelis voted on October 27, the first elections since the October 7, 2023 Hamas attacks. With roughly 42% of ballots counted by Wednesday morning, Netanyahu's Likud-led bloc held a 57-47 seat lead over the opposition. Likud itself won approximately 21-22% of the vote. The coalition math is unresolved -- 61 seats are needed for a governing majority -- and opposition leader Yair Golan has called on anti-Netanyahu factions to seek cooperation with Arab parties to reach that threshold. Prediction markets placed Netanyahu as next prime minister at 41% as of Friday, reflecting genuine uncertainty about whether he can assemble a working coalition. Final results are due by November 4. The first Israeli election since the Gaza war began does not resolve the broader strategic question of what Israel's postwar political direction looks like; it creates a period of coalition negotiation inside which that direction will be partially determined.

The US-Iran ceasefire sits at 93% probability of continuing through October 31. The OFAC GL FF-1 issuance the day before the resolution date is the most concrete signal that the US is actively managing, not merely observing, the ceasefire window. Whether the license is a prelude to a more durable framework will become visible in the next 30 days through additional OFAC actions or the absence of them.

VIP flight patterns (ADS-B data, Friday snapshot) show US military airlift callsigns (PAT, JEDI series) active over the eastern US and Gulf Coast. Belgian Air Force callsigns (JAF series) were active across western Europe with Atlantic routing, consistent with NATO support posture for the Ukraine theater. No anomalous routing into the Middle East or Indo-Pacific was visible.

Ukraine theater frontline data (DeepStateMap, 522 polygons, current as of Friday) shows no dramatic territorial change visible in the polygon counts. The Russian internal news feed sustained 684-811 items per day across the week -- elevated and consistent -- with recurring accounts of conscripts deployed under conditions differing from their stated contracts, and casualty reporting in the Zaporizhzhia and Donetsk sectors. The Ukrainian internal feed ran 66-68 items per day, notably smaller in volume, which is typical of a defensive operational posture with more limited domestic reporting of frontline specifics.

## Commentary digest

The week's commentary cohered around three themes: artificial intelligence economics, tariff regime sustainability, and macroeconomic regime identification.

The Conversable Economist's "All About Tariffs" (October 28) is a comprehensive reckoning with the Trump tariff program since Liberation Day 2025. The argument is not that tariffs are politically unpopular; it is that they function as a supply shock -- raising input costs, displacing established trade relationships, and feeding inflation in ways that complicate any coherent monetary policy response. The piece connects directly to the FOMC's 10-2 hold and the bear-steepening of the curve: if the Fed is partly fighting tariff-induced inflation, rate hikes worsen growth without addressing the supply-side cause.

The same author's "A Supply-Shock Era?" (carried through the week) poses the regime-identification question directly: is the current macro environment demand-driven or supply-driven? The distinction matters enormously for policy. The FOMC's 10-2 vote, the bear steepening, and the ECB's hold-while-tightening credit posture all suggest policymakers are not yet committed to either answer.

Tyler Cowen's Chronicle of Higher Education interview (Marginal Revolution, October 30) on AI and education proposes an AI-native course design -- one class per student run entirely through AI, with a professor grading workflow rather than answers. This is a leading indicator of the institutional debate that is approximately 18-24 months from landing in university administrative calendars.

The World Bank's World Development Report 2026 on AI in development (Conversable Economist, carried from October 23 through the week) frames AI access as the most consequential deficit-reduction mechanism for low-income countries, particularly in skills acquisition. This is the multilateral context for the semiconductor export control watch area: what the US restricts at the frontier diffuses via lower-compute alternatives into exactly the development corridors the World Bank is describing.

"Brazil's Economic Problems, in a Nutshell" (Marginal Revolution, October 26) is a useful reference point for the EM equities picture: EEM underperformed all week except Thursday, and the Brazil-specific political economy issues (fiscal expansion into a tightening global rate environment, institutional independence concerns) are a proxy for a broader set of EM vulnerabilities heading into a potential US December hike.

## Cross-section themes

**Monetary divergence is no longer a forecast; it is a current condition.** The Fed held with hawkish dissenters, the ECB held with a departing president, and the 2yr-fed funds spread of 80 basis points implies the market sees US rates moving materially higher. The ECB succession now becomes the dominant European macro event for the next quarter.

**AI is finding its cost curve.** Google's earnings hit is the visible manifestation of a dynamic that the Conversable Economist's AI capital buildout piece identified in abstract: financing AI infrastructure at 5% rates, in competition with the same AI's tendency to cannibalize high-margin revenue streams, produces a very different investment calculus than financing it at 2% in a period of rising revenues. Both the Google advertising story and the supply-shock framing of tariffs point toward a monetization cliff that markets priced only partially this week.

**The midterm is the policy fulcrum.** A 94% probability of Democratic House control, plus 70% for a full sweep, represents the single most consequential binary in the near-term policy space. A full Democratic sweep would invert the legislative dynamic on tariffs (Canada, wind turbines, and the broader Liberation Day framework would face immediate legislative challenge), executive action scope, and the debt ceiling. The FEC fundraising asymmetry in Texas, Georgia, Ohio, Michigan, and North Carolina reinforces the prediction market signal and suggests the underlying voter environment matches the money environment.

**Sanctions are being used as ceasefire infrastructure.** OFAC's GL FF-1 for Iran, the amended blocking/rejecting FAQ, and the North Korea/DRC simultaneous designations in one week are not uncoordinated. The Iran license is the most important piece: it is the financial scaffold of a potential longer ceasefire. Whether it holds depends on what the parties signal in the first two weeks of November.

**Oil absorbed a large shock and held.** The 4.32% single-day spike that fully retraced by Friday tells you the market prices Middle East supply risk as short-term and bounded. At USO 168.19 by Friday close, the level supports US energy sector fiscal assumptions but remains elevated enough to keep inflationary pressure on transportation and logistics costs.

## What to watch -- next 14 days

**November 4: US midterm elections.** The single most market-relevant event in the window. A Democratic House and Senate (the Polymarket modal scenario) would change the tariff, appropriations, and executive authority baseline immediately. Texas and Georgia Senate races are the bellwether contests for whether the Democratic sweep thesis extends to the Senate.

**November 4: US-Iran ceasefire resolution.** The Polymarket contract resolves the same day as the election. Whether the ceasefire holds into November -- and whether additional OFAC licenses follow in its wake -- is the primary geopolitical signpost.

**ECB succession signaling.** With Lagarde departing February 7, the next six weeks will surface trial balloons, leaks, and EU-internal negotiations about her successor. German and French economic policy preferences will appear through candidate positioning; watch for whether the successor is seen as more hawkish (German preference) or more accommodative (French and Southern European preference).

**FOMC December meeting (December 9).** Given the 10-2 October hold and the bear steepening, December is live. A hike would push effective fed funds above 4% and widen the transatlantic gap with the ECB at 2.5%, further strengthening the dollar and increasing pressure on EM sovereigns with dollar-denominated debt.

**Israel coalition formation.** Final results are due November 4. Formal coalition talks begin after certification. Whether Netanyahu can assemble a 61-seat coalition without the Arab parties -- or whether Golan's opposition bloc manages to do so with them -- will determine Israel's postwar political direction on Gaza reconstruction, West Bank policy, and US-Israel relations.

**Alphabet follow-on and hyperscaler earnings read-through.** The Friday selloff sets up a two-week debate about whether Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon face analogous AI-cannibalizes-revenue dynamics. Their earnings and forward guidance in early November are the next data points.

**UK defence spending.** The autumn statement is the likely next policy milestone. Watch whether the government names a specific percentage-of-GDP target.
