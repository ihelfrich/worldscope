## Headline of the week

A single data print rewrote the monetary policy consensus in roughly six hours. The Bureau of Labor Statistics released the October consumer-price index on Tuesday morning, November 10, and the numbers were above every major forecast: headline CPI +0.4% month-over-month, core CPI +0.3% (consensus expected +0.2%). Because the 2025 government shutdown left no October 2025 baseline, no annual rate was published, so the market had to work from the sequential print alone. It worked fast. By Tuesday evening, the probability of a December FOMC rate hike had reached 84% on prediction markets, up from roughly 20% the prior Monday when no-change was still a live option. It closed the week at 88% for a +25bp hike, with 9% pricing +50bp and no meaningful probability left on a cut or a hold. That regime shift in monetary-policy pricing was the structural fact of the week: everything else -- bond sell-off, yield-curve flattening, gold slide, dollar grind, equities holding -- followed from it.

## Macro: how the picture moved

The CPI print turned a gradually firming rates environment into a sharp repricing. In the FRED data carried by the daily bundles, the two-year Treasury opened the week at 4.46% (data as of Nov 5) and closed it at 4.73% (data as of Nov 10), a 27 basis-point move in five business days. The ten-year moved from 4.84% to 4.98%, a 14-basis-point move, and the thirty-year from 5.25% to 5.35%. The 10-year/2-year spread, the most-watched recession indicator, compressed from 35 basis points to 24 basis points across the week (data as of Nov 12). That is not a signal of imminent inversion, but it is a meaningful flattening: the front end is repricing the Fed more aggressively than the long end is repricing the inflation terminal. The curve is telling you the market thinks the Fed will overshoot, or at least tighten enough to slow growth, without yet betting on a full reflation of the long run.

Fed Governor Christopher Waller spoke at the Bank of England Watchers Conference in London on November 10, the same day as the CPI release, in a speech titled "The Economic Outlook and Some Thoughts on Labor Market Dynamism." Governor Michelle Bowman also spoke that day at the Council on Foreign Relations in New York on "The U.S. Economy and Considerations for Monetary Policy." The calendar data does not carry transcripts, so the content of those speeches cannot be directly quoted here, but their coincidence with the CPI print made them closely watched. Fed Vice Chair Michael Barr spoke at Yale on November 12 on "American Inequality and the Future of AI." The Fed also published a revised tentative FOMC meeting schedule for 2027 on November 12, a procedural step that nonetheless drew attention in the context of an institution about to begin a hiking cycle in December.

The labor market reading that underpins the hike case: unemployment is 4.1% as of October (FRED vintage), nonfarm payrolls stand at 159.3 million. JOLTS job openings are 7.5 million as of September, still elevated by historical standards. Headline CPI as of October is 337.5 on the index; September was 336.3. Core CPI (ex food and energy) is 339.9 as of October. Core PCE, the Fed's preferred measure, was last reported at 130.9 as of September -- the November read will not arrive until after the December 8-9 meeting. The Fed is hiking into an economy that is slowing but not broken. That is precisely the analytical difficulty: the case for holding is not absent; the market has simply decided the case for hiking is stronger.

One commodity to flag separately: WTI crude ended the week at $89.62 (data as of Nov 11), and the USO oil ETF rose 3.25% on November 12 alone. Oil is rising through a tightening cycle, which complicates the inflation picture going into December.

## Markets: rotation and dispersion

The SPY opened the week at 735.29 (Nov 6 close) and ended Thursday at 757.12, a 3.0% advance on the week despite a -0.35% session on November 12. The Nasdaq 100 (QQQ) gained 4.9% from 656.10 to 688.17. Those are strong returns for a week in which ten-year yields rose fourteen basis points; the equity market's ability to shrug off a significant rates repricing suggests that the earnings-growth narrative is currently winning against the discount-rate narrative for large-cap technology and growth.

Dispersion matters here. The Russell 2000 (IWM) advanced only 1.7% from 273.67 to 278.44, and was notably weak on November 12 (down 1.64% on that session alone). Small-cap underperformance in a rising-rate week is a classic pattern: smaller companies carry more floating-rate debt, have thinner margins and are more sensitive to growth slowdowns. The gap between QQQ (+4.9%) and IWM (+1.7%) is a rotation signal worth watching.

International divergence was pronounced. MSCI EAFE (EFA) gained 2.1% (101.89 to 104.01), participating in the risk-on tone. But China large-cap (FXI) fell from 34.95 to 33.48, a 4.2% decline over the week. That underperformance reflects the Nvidia/Jensen Huang spy allegations intensifying ahead of APEC (see geopolitical section), continued uncertainty about the January trade truce deadline, and persistent concerns about China's domestic demand.

The fixed-income picture tells the sharper story. The long-bond ETF (TLT) fell 1.48% on November 12 alone. Credit spreads widened modestly: HYG (high yield) fell 0.48% and LQD (investment grade) fell 0.88% on that session. The VXX volatility ETF rose 3.15% on November 12, confirming that the bond-market stress was beginning to bleed into cross-asset hedging activity by week's end. Bitcoin (BITO) fell 4.33% -- a sharp reversal in a week when equity risk appetite was generally positive, suggesting crypto-specific selling unrelated to the macro bid.

Gold (GLD) fell 2.82% on November 12, the largest single-day move in the commodity complex. Silver (SLV) fell 3.28%. Gold typically sells off when real rates rise sharply and the dollar firms; both conditions are present. The EUR/USD stood at 1.1154 as of November 6 (FRED vintage), and the DXY proxy (UUP ETF) was up 0.24% on November 12 and up meaningfully week-over-week. The USD/JPY stood at 158.23 as of November 6, a level that keeps the Bank of Japan's intervention calculation live.

## Politics and policy: what moved in Washington and elsewhere

The midterm elections are over and the leadership reckoning is arriving. Within 24 hours of November 13, Chris Van Hollen and Elissa Slotkin declined to back Chuck Schumer at a post-election gathering of Senate Democrats. Van Hollen is "considering" a formal challenge for Minority Leader (inference: this is an open contest, not a coronation). Reports put the number of viable Democratic challengers at a half-dozen. Schumer faces pressure from progressives who view his prior collaboration with the administration as disqualifying and from newer members who see a generational transition as overdue. The Senate Democratic leadership contest will likely resolve in the week of seating in January.

The FEC fundraising data, which was unchanged between Sunday and Friday (carry-forward, as the manifest noted), shows the cycle-to-date picture: James Talarico (D-TX) tops the Senate leaderboard at $146.4 million raised, Jon Ossoff (D-GA) at $136.6 million, Sherrod Brown (D-OH) at $68.4 million, Abdul El-Sayed (D-MI) at $53.8 million. The scale of Texas and Georgia fundraising suggests those races were genuinely competitive this cycle. Trump Media shares fell to a record low after midterm results, per a natural-news aggregator item in the conflict feed (secondary-source quality; treat as directional signal only, not precision data).

## Geopolitical signal

**Ukraine.** The pace of combat remained high: the conflict feed logged 168 daily clashes on November 13, with the heaviest concentrations at Kostiantynivka (12), Pokrovsk (11), and Huliaipole (7). ISW assessments ran every day of the week. On November 11, explosions were reported near the Omsk oil refinery in Siberia, a facility processing roughly 20 million tons per year. Liveuamap simultaneously logged Russia losing two helicopters (a Ka-52 and a Mi-8) overnight in the Leningrad Oblast, geographically distant from the front -- a possible but unconfirmed indication of a broader strike pattern.

The diplomatic track moved simultaneously at the European Political Community summit in Dublin (November 12) and an EU-US-Ukraine coordination session in Abu Dhabi. The EPC hosted roughly 50 leaders; Zelensky met with Trump envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner on the sidelines. Per an Irish news analysis, that meeting drew more attention than any other at the summit. The conflict feed for November 13 also carried an item noting "Kushner and Witkoff Meet Putin" and "RU Envoys Carry Fake F-Line Maps to Peace Table" (secondary-source, GDELT aggregation, unverified against primary sources). The EU is debating who should represent it at talks it has been excluded from so far; Meloni proposed a formal EU-level decision at December's summit. Trump is seeking to arrange a Putin meeting at next week's APEC in Shenzhen.

**Iran.** Trump said this week he would "not hesitate to strike Iran" if US officials are killed, adding that "they will not exist." Polymarket priced the Israel-Iran ceasefire holding through November 15 at 97% as of Friday; the ceasefire appears to be holding despite elevated rhetorical temperature. ISW published a daily Iran update on November 11.

**China/Nvidia.** The Jensen Huang spy allegations are the most consequential tech-geopolitical story this week. US intelligence reportedly concluded that Alice Huang, a Chinese businesswoman whose firm purchased large volumes of Nvidia AI chips, was an asset of China's Ministry of State Security. Jensen Huang acknowledged a personal relationship following the reporting. Trump said he believed Huang was unaware of her affiliation. The case raises three concerns that go beyond the individuals: the degree to which Chinese government-affiliated buyers penetrated the chip supply chain, whether Huang's close relationship with Trump impeded export-control enforcement, and what the revelation means for APEC negotiations in Shenzhen next week. A parallel prosecution of two Americans and two Chinese nationals for illegally exporting Nvidia GPUs ran alongside the Huang story in the week's coverage.

**New Zealand.** The election remained too close to call as of Sunday; coalition negotiations are expected to run several weeks.

**Yemen/Houthis.** Houthi forces were active in Lahij Governorate per the Liveuamap feed on November 12.

## Commentary digest

The Substack/RSS feed this week was dominated by Marginal Revolution and the Conversable Economist. Five pieces connect directly to this brief's themes.

Tyler Cowen, "End the State Veto on Power Lines" (Marginal Revolution, November 9): Argues that natural gas pipelines can cross state lines on a single federal permit while electricity requires a gauntlet of state approvals. This is directly relevant to the energy-infrastructure bottleneck that makes the oil-price story more structurally sticky than a simple demand narrative would suggest.

Tyler Cowen, "Spying Makes a Comeback" (Marginal Revolution, November 11): Describes "a wealthy, powerful man making an irresponsible decision" whose story, below the surface, is about the structural comeback of human-intelligence operations. Without naming Huang directly, this reads as an editorial contextualization of the week's dominant technology-security story.

Zidar and Zwick, "The Everywhere Rent Seekers" (via Marginal Revolution, November 12): Argues that the political power of billionaires differs from millionaires in important structural ways. Relevant context for understanding why the Nvidia chip-export debate is contested on Capitol Hill despite bipartisan sentiment against technology transfer to China.

Timothy Taylor, "Social Security Reform Looms Ahead" (Conversable Economist, pre-week): The 2026 midterms just concluded. Social Security's long-run actuarial gap is now closer to requiring legislation before the end of this Congress. Taylor traces the trajectory from the 1983 fix forward. This is a slow-moving background fiscal constraint that the December FOMC decision and rising long-term rates (5.35% thirty-year Treasury) will accelerate.

Iñaki Aldasoro et al., "How Currencies Have Come and Gone: 1850-2026" (Conversable Economist, November 9, via BIS): 568 currencies issued since 1850; 405 discontinued. In a week when the yen is at 158.23 and the dollar is firming on rate-hike expectations, the historical perspective on currency durability is analytically useful.

## Cross-section themes (the synthesis layer)

**Theme 1: A December hike as a regime boundary.** This is not just a rates story. The 88% probability of a December hike sits at the intersection of four feeds this week: macro (hot CPI, strong labor), markets (front-end yields +27bp, curve flattening), forecasts (the probability trajectory from 20% no-change to 88% hike in three days), and central bank calendar (Waller and Bowman speeches on the day of the print). The Fed is hiking into an oil price above $89. The November CPI print on December 10 -- the day after the FOMC decision -- becomes the most consequential single data point for the first quarter of 2027.

**Theme 2: The Ukraine peace process is bifurcating.** The US channel (Witkoff-Kushner to Moscow, then to Dublin to meet Zelensky) and the EU channel (seeking a seat at a table from which it was excluded in Abu Dhabi) are running in parallel but not in coordination. Russia's envoys reportedly brought misrepresentative frontline maps to talks (conflict feed, unverified). The gap between the American "deal" framing and the Ukrainian "sovereignty" framing has not closed. APEC next week adds a potential Trump-Putin dimension that could accelerate or complicate the Abu Dhabi process.

**Theme 3: The semiconductor supply chain as a full-spectrum battleground.** The Huang/China-spy story, the two-American/two-Chinese GPU smuggling prosecution, the chip-export-control debate that his relationship with Trump allegedly impeded, and the FXI underperformance ahead of APEC all point at the same underlying contest: who controls the physical stack of AI computation. This is now a story that runs through counterintelligence, trade negotiation, executive enforcement and market pricing simultaneously.

**Theme 4: Post-midterm Democratic realignment has begun.** The Senate leadership contest (Van Hollen/Slotkin vs. Schumer), the scale of fundraising in Texas and Georgia, and Trump Media at record lows collectively suggest the 2026 midterms produced meaningful Democratic gains. The party is now working out whether Schumer-era accommodation is the model going forward or whether a more confrontational posture -- aligned with new senators from competitive states -- replaces it. This contest will shape the Senate's institutional behavior toward the FOMC, fiscal policy, and foreign policy through 2028.
