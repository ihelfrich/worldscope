## Headline of the week

The week's organizing logic was compression: three slow-building pressures that had been accumulating for months each reached a visible inflection point within forty-eight hours. On Thursday, CIA Director John Ratcliffe flew to Moscow — the first confirmed visit by a sitting CIA director to the Russian capital since the war in Ukraine began — to warn the Kremlin against testing NATO with a limited military incursion that U.S. intelligence assessed as a credible possibility. That same day, Ukrainian President Zelensky's chief of staff said September talks between Kyiv and Moscow, with U.S. participation, were "possible." And on Friday, Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivered a hawkish first Jackson Hole speech, calling inflation "concerning" and signaling that "more work" may be needed — a message markets read as a live probability of a September rate hike. Gold fell 3.2 percent on the news. The throughline across all three events: regimes that had been held in suspension by a combination of political will and ambiguity are now being tested, and the results will arrive before the November 3 midterms.

## Macro: how the picture moved

The week's macro snapshot is a study in two competing gravitational fields. On one side: months of accumulated war-driven energy inflation that pushed the 10-year Treasury yield to an intraweek peak of 4.74 percent on August 21 and kept the 30-year above 5.25 percent for most of the week. On the other: a slow but real easing in crude prices and a VIX that drifted down from 16.0 to 14.5 before Friday's slight reversal.

By end of week the 10-year settled at 4.66 percent (2-year at 4.19 percent, 30-year at 5.18 percent), a spread of 47 basis points on the 10-2, which remains comfortably positive and is not flagging recession. The Fed funds effective rate held at 3.63 percent, within the 3.50–3.75 percent target band set when the FOMC voted 9-3 to hold in July — three hawks already dissenting in favor of a hike.

Then came the Warsh speech. The Fed chair's Jackson Hole address was his first as chair and his clearest signal yet: inflation progress is insufficient, financial conditions are "not broadly restrictive," and the 2 percent PCE target is non-negotiable. Markets moved immediately. September hike probability, which the bundle's prediction-market feed pegged at 30 percent as of Thursday, was repriced above 50 percent in the minutes after the speech. Gold was the bluntest instrument of that repricing, falling $13.66 on GLD (3.2 percent) to close at $408.94. The dollar proxy (UUP) rose 0.57 percent to 28.18 — a multi-week high.

This is not a fully hawkish regime yet: long bonds (TLT) actually gained slightly on the week, from 82.05 to 82.89, as markets seem to read the Warsh threat as front-loading that shortens the overall cycle rather than permanently lifting the long end. But the J.P. Morgan assessment widely circulated this week — that July's hold "lowered the bar" for a September hike — is now market consensus. Warsh's public refusal to provide forward guidance makes that ambiguity the instrument of policy itself.

WTI crude (DCOILWTICO) fell from around $86.48 in the early part of the week to $83.90 by August 25, with a sharp single-day drop in the USO proxy on August 26 (USO -4.58 percent) before a partial recovery Thursday and flat close Friday. The oil move is not obviously Iran-driven — Hormuz physical flows have not materially changed this week — and is more likely a combination of demand softness signals and speculative unwinding. Both Venezuela and Iran blockade-probability markets remain in the low single digits for near-term end.

SOFR held at 3.64 percent. M2 was $23.2 trillion as of July. The Fed balance sheet was $6.73 trillion as of August 26, declining slowly. Core PCE (130.66) and core CPI (336.8) are the key prints to watch for September — neither has dropped to levels that would give Warsh the cover to stand down.

## Politics and policy: Washington and the midterm countdown

The week's most consequential domestic document was a single line in the Federal Register entry from August 26: the Postal Service is amending the Domestic Mail Manual "regarding the transmission of mail-in ballots for federal elections." The entry arrived alongside a separate USPS statement confirming that "new mail ballot rules tied to Trump order are in effect." This is the administrative infrastructure for the administration's approach to the November 3 election, and it is now live. California's Governor Newsom announced the state is "preparing for scrutiny" — the state-level counteroffensive is underway.

The FEC data across the week tells the fundraising story for the midterms. Jon Ossoff (D-GA, Senate) leads all candidates at $97.99 million raised this cycle, with $38.26 million cash on hand — suggesting he is treating Georgia as the battleground of the cycle. James Talarico (D-TX, Senate) is at $68.56 million raised, a figure that reflects genuine national enthusiasm for a Texas pick-up scenario. Sherrod Brown (D-OH) and Roy Cooper (D-NC) follow. The Republican top-line shows Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) at $20.98 million raised — he is spending down ($12.6M disbursed) ahead of what the forecasting models are treating as a difficult November. Graham (R-SC) is in deficit by $10 million relative to disbursements.

Polling aggregates as of mid-August showed Democrats with an 8-point generic congressional ballot lead. Polymarket and Silver Bulletin models are converging on Democrats as favorites to retake the House; Senate is closer. Trump and Vance headlined a Republican midterm convention this week — the branding of a resistance to the headwinds is already underway.

On the administration's regulatory front, the Federal Register this week was dominated by OPM corrections to the August 3 reduction-in-force, suitability, and probationary-period rules — the machinery of the civil service restructuring that began earlier this year is now being technically corrected, not unwound. The EEOC also proposed streamlining federal sector EEO complaint rules, reducing the process by which federal workers can challenge adverse actions. The SEC proposed new rules for crypto investment contracts as a "tailored offering regime." BIS made entity-list revisions (two addresses removed).

The Supreme Court docket shows Trump v. California has been accepted — no further detail in the feed, but the trajectory of state-versus-federal confrontation on election procedures, environmental policy, and immigration suggests this case is part of a broader SCOTUS term shaping up as one of the most consequential in the decade.

One item with staying power: the administration's attempt to remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook. Her attorney this week said the grounds are "untried and unproven allegations of criminal wrongdoing." This collides directly with Warsh's hawkish stance — Trump has repeatedly demanded lower rates. The Cook removal effort, if it proceeds, is a slow-motion challenge to central bank independence that has not yet priced into longer-term rate expectations.

## Sanctions and legal

"Operation Economic Outcast" — OFAC's language, not an analyst label — launched publicly on August 24 when Treasury indefinitely suspended five Iranian Transactions and Sanctions Regulations general licenses, including 31 CFR 560.544, 560.550, and 560.554, and Iran General Licenses F and G. These had authorized educational activities by U.S. persons, noncommercial personal remittances to Iran, and conference-related services. The suspension is effective immediately and indefinite in duration.

The timing is precise: the 60-day window opened by the June 17 MOU between Trump and President Pezeshkian — which had temporarily removed the naval blockade in exchange for Iranian commitments on commercial passage — expired in mid-August. With no deal formalized, OFAC has moved to re-close the perimeter. The Federal Register on August 27 added two new Iran-related general licenses (AA and BB) and published a sector determination under Executive Order 13902 — these are the legal scaffolding for the re-tightened sanctions posture.

Iran's foreign trade situation is acute. The bundle's foreign news carried multiple items this week on Iran "scrambling to sustain trade as the U.S. threatens to sanction countries that refuse to comply." The Indian-flagged company ALCHEMICAL SOLUTIONS PRIVATE LIMITED appeared on the OFAC SDN list this week — a signal that secondary sanctions are being used actively against third-country entities facilitating Iran trade. The U.S. blockade has cost Iran an estimated $500 million per day, with DoD estimating $4.8 billion in oil revenue losses by May 1.

The Hormuz strait remains de facto contested. U.S. allies warned this week that despite Trump's earlier claim that the strait had been cleared of mines, "many mines are likely to have drifted due to sea currents." The physical risk is not resolved.

For Ukraine-related sanctions, the week's consolidated feed showed continued multi-jurisdictional sanctions pressure on Russian financial institutions — Sberbank Europe, GPB Global Resources, MIR Business Bank, Tinkoff, RUSAL-adjacent entities. The EU's four-country push (Netherlands, Poland, Spain, Sweden) to reopen the debate on using Russia's frozen assets for Ukraine reached Brussels but did not move to a formal decision.

On the CIT tariff docket, no new major rulings surfaced, but Canada's partial reversal on counter-tariffs — excluding seafood and fish from its $20 billion retaliatory list — reflects the ongoing negotiation dynamic around the U.S.-Canada trade war.

## Geopolitical signal

The week's geopolitical architecture turned on two axes: the Iran endgame and the Russia-NATO escalation ladder.

**Iran**: Six months into the conflict, the bundle's foreign news carried the clearest summary of the strategic situation: both sides have dug in, both believing time is on their side. The U.S. naval blockade (originally imposed April 13) cost Iran heavily but also began draining the U.S. Navy budget at a rate that Pentagon documents describe as unsustainable at current tempo. The June 17 MOU gave a 60-day breathing room that has now expired without a lasting agreement. Iran and Qatar held Hormuz talks in Tehran on August 27 — the involvement of Qatar, the historical channel for Iran-U.S. back-channel communication, is significant. The prediction markets (2 percent yes price for "US announces end of Iranian blockade by August 31") confirm no near-term resolution.

**Russia-NATO**: The Ratcliffe Moscow visit was the week's most consequential geopolitical event. U.S. intelligence had assessed that Russia, weakened in Ukraine, might attempt a limited provocative action against NATO — most likely a Baltic nation — ranging from a cyberattack to a small-scale incursion. Ratcliffe flew to Moscow, met with SVR chief Sergei Naryshkin, and delivered the warning. Russia confirmed the meeting but said it was "working level." Putin did not meet Ratcliffe. Trump then downplayed the trip publicly.

The simultaneous signals are: Russia is probing the edges; the U.S. is delivering direct deterrent messaging; and Ukraine's Budanov is floating September talks. These are not contradictory — they are the operational pattern of a war approaching a negotiated envelope without either side being ready to formalize it. The UK's approval of Storm Shadow technology transfer to Ukraine for indigenous production complicates the picture by giving Kyiv a long-run capability uplift even as talks are floated. Russia's explicit threat to strike UK military targets "inside and outside Ukraine" is the counter-signal.

On Ukraine's battlefield, Zelensky publicly set a target of 1,000 drone strikes per day into Russia. The FIRMS thermal anomaly data in the bundle continues to show heat signatures in eastern Ukraine and western Russia consistent with active strikes. Ukrainian forces struck Russian airfields including Millerovo (Rostov region). Russia struck the Ukrainian-Moldovan border crossing — a pressure on the Chisinau link.

**China**: The Wang Yi-U.S. Ambassador meeting language ("clear interference for high-level contacts") suggests a deliberate effort to keep the bilateral stable ahead of what both sides likely recognize as a turbulent autumn. The AVIC (Aviation Industry Corporation of China) leadership change — Wei Yingbiao named head — at China's largest defense aerospace conglomerate is worth tracking; leadership transitions at major defense enterprises often precede procurement or export shifts. The Jiangxi Governor Ye Jianchun's corruption fall is part of Xi's ongoing political consolidation, not a new regime shift.

**Norway**: King Harald V died this week at 89. Crown Prince Haakon takes the throne. In the context of a week dominated by NATO escalation concerns, the leadership transition in one of the most strategically positioned NATO members (Norwegian Arctic, oil sector, intelligence cooperation) is worth noting as a continuity marker — Norway's foreign policy posture is unlikely to change, but there will be a period of institutional focus on the succession.

**Nepal**: The glacial-lake outburst flood on the Langtang Lirung massif killed more than 580 people with 2,500 missing as of Friday. The humanitarian response is significant; the geopolitical angle is that the disaster occurred on the Nepal-China border, with Chinese media initially tracking 558 missing. Climate-driven GLOF events at high altitude are a slow-moving signal across the Himalayan arc.

**"Even Sand Isn't Infinite"** (Conversable Economist, multiple days): The persistence of this post across three days of feeds suggests it struck a chord — the argument that industrial-grade sand is a genuine supply-constraint story connects to the broader critical-materials theme running through this week's semiconductor, data-center, and defense equipment discussions.

## Cross-section themes: the synthesis layer

**Theme 1 — Iran as the forcing function across every regime.** Iran this week appeared simultaneously in: OFAC sanctions actions (Operation Economic Outcast), Federal Register (general license suspensions, sector determinations), geopolitics (Hormuz talks, blockade budget drain), markets (oil down despite Iran tensions), prediction markets (2% blockade-end probability), and foreign news (secondary sanctions on Indian companies). The six-month stalemate is not a frozen conflict — it is actively reshaping the U.S. fiscal, naval, and sanctions architecture. The 60-day MOU window expired without resolution, which is itself a data point: there is no diplomatic off-ramp in sight.

**Theme 2 — Russia in three feeds simultaneously.** Russia showed up this week in: Ukraine theater (drone strikes, airfield hits), VIP flights (Belgian military jets in Morocco and Algerian coast), geopolitics (Ratcliffe warning, NATO escalation assessment), Russian internal news (RUSAL mothballing Pikalevo capacity, Yabloko's broadcast cut on VK), and the courtlistener feed (Ukrainian drone-law developments). The RUSAL item is underappreciated: mothballing aluminum capacity in Pikalevo — a city where 2009 plant closures triggered protests Putin personally had to defuse — is an economic-stress signal that the sanctions perimeter is biting the Russian industrial base in politically sensitive ways.

**Theme 3 — Warsh, the Fed, and the Trump confrontation.** Three independent signals converged this week on the same stress point: Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech; the administration's attempt to remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook (whose attorney publicly pushed back); and the prediction-market repricing of September from 30% to 56% hike probability in twenty-four hours. These are not coincidences — they are a visible conflict between executive pressure for lower rates and a Fed chair who appears to be using the absence of forward guidance as a shield against political interference. The October CPI print (releasing in mid-November, after the midterms) will be the decisive data.

**Theme 4 — Election infrastructure as regulatory action.** The USPS mail ballot rule change (effective now), the EEOC's proposed streamlining of federal sector EEO complaints, OPM's corrections to the reduction-in-force machinery, and Trump v. California's Supreme Court acceptance all appear in the same week. Taken individually, each is an administrative action. Taken together, they sketch the federal government's institutional posture going into the November election. The Democratic fundraising numbers and the 8-point generic ballot lead are the electoral response signal.

**Theme 5 — NATO's eastern flank and the AI-defense intersection.** The UK-Ukraine AI defense agreement signed this week (visible in the GDELT/Ukraine feed) sits alongside Bulgaria's purchase of seven minehunter ships from Belgium and the Netherlands, the Pentagon's Europe troop-deployment review (delivering options to Hegseth by November 6), and Eastern Flank Commissioners urging von der Leyen to appoint a Russia-coordination role. The former Ukrainian Defense Minister Fedorov becoming an advisor to Italy's Defense Ministry is a small but telling personnel signal: Ukraine's defense-tech expertise is dispersing into NATO structures. The week's AI-defense investment cluster is a quiet but real capability-building story running beneath the diplomatic headlines.
