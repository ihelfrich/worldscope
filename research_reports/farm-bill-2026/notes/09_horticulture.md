# Title X — Horticulture, Marketing, and Regulatory Reform — Notes

## Title-level summary
Title X reauthorizes USDA horticulture, marketing, and regulatory programs through FY2031 and pairs the extensions with substantive policy reforms. On horticulture, it extends Specialty Crop Block Grants (and bars cost-sharing/matching requirements), the urban agriculture office (converting its pilot into a permanent program), and—most significantly—overhauls hemp regulation by creating an "industrial hemp" production designation with relaxed sampling/testing and shifting the THC test to total tetrahydrocannabinol (including THCA). A new freestanding APHIS pilot program lets up to 75 vetted "responsible parties" move genetically engineered microorganisms in interstate commerce without permits. The Marketing subtitle expands the Local Agriculture Market Program (adds "food hub" definition, simplified small-grant applications) and makes multiple reforms to organic programs—organic milk cost data, organic technical assistance, modernized inspection rules allowing virtual inspections every three years for lower-risk U.S. operations, and a study to build risk-based National Organic Program oversight. The Regulatory Reform subtitle reshapes FIFRA by excluding plant biostimulants/nutritional chemicals/certain plant-incorporated protectants from pesticide regulation, mandating EPA–USDA coordination and economic analysis on risk-mitigation and Endangered Species Act measures, plus a 5-year safe harbor for aerial wildfire-retardant discharges and a new USDA Office of Biotechnology Policy. Notably, this title contains no California Proposition 12 / state-law preemption provision.

## Section-by-section

### Sec. 10001 — Specialty crop block grants
- **Does:** Extends Specialty Crop Block Grant authority from 2023 to 2031; reframes grants to be made "through priorities established annually by State program administrators in consultation with specialty crop producers and producer groups"; restructures subsection (c) and adds a flat prohibition on any cost-sharing or matching requirement on awards or sub-awards; rewrites subsection (e) State plan requirements (must identify lead agency and indicate how funds enhance competitiveness and how producer outreach/consultation is achieved).
- **Amends:** Section 101 of the Specialty Crops Competitiveness Act of 2004 (7 U.S.C. 1621 note; Pub. L. 108–465).
- **Numbers:** No new dollar figure stated; new bar on cost-share/match (effectively 0% match).
- **Dates:** Reauthorization year changed 2023 → 2031.
- **Type:** EXTEND / REFORM.
- **Affected:** Specialty crop producers and producer groups (winners — bigger consultative role, no match burden); State departments of agriculture (must run annual priority-setting and update plans).
- **Flag:** Explicit ban on any cost-sharing or matching requirement is a notable producer-friendly change.

### Sec. 10003 — Office of Urban Agriculture and Innovative Production

- **Amends:** Section 222 of the Department of Agriculture Reorganization Act of 1994 (7 U.S.C. 6923).

### Sec. 10005 — Hemp production
- **Does:** Major hemp regulatory overhaul.
  - State/Tribal plans (7 U.S.C. 1639p): adds a required procedure for producers to designate production type as "only industrial hemp" or "hemp grown for any purpose other than industrial hemp." Switches the testing standard from "delta-9 tetrahydrocannabinol concentration" to "total tetrahydrocannabinol concentration (including tetrahydrocannabinolic acid)." Allows plans to include (notwithstanding standard sampling): visual inspections, performance-based sampling, certified seed, or similar procedures for producers electing "only industrial hemp"; and to eliminate the 10-year felony-drug-conviction ineligibility period for such producers. Adds inspection-of-industrial-hemp-producer rules: producers using reduced sampling must provide documentation of clear intent to produce only industrial hemp (seed tag, sales contract, FSA report, harvest technique/inspection); failure triggers testing.
  - Sets the THC limit at total tetrahydrocannabinol (including THCA) of not more than 0.3 percent in the plant (replacing delta-9 ... 0.3 percent).
  - Reporting/enforcement: requires immediate report to the Attorney General (and State/Tribal chief law enforcement) if a producer violated the plan with culpable mental state greater than negligence, OR produced a crop inconsistent with the "only industrial hemp" designation. Adds a 5-year program ineligibility for any person who knowingly produces a crop inconsistent with the "only industrial hemp" designation.
  - USDA plan (7 U.S.C. 1639q): mirrors the same designation procedure, total-THC testing standard, optional reduced-sampling and felony-ineligibility-elimination procedures, and industrial hemp inspection requirements at the federal level; adds reporting of production-type designation and requires laboratory certificate of analysis for disposed hemp.
  - Administration (7 U.S.C. 1639r): renames section heading to "Administration, Regulations, and Guidelines"; directs the Secretary, in consultation with the DEA Administrator, to establish a process for USDA to issue certificates of accreditation to hemp-testing laboratories.
- **Amends:** Sections 297B, 297C, and 297D of the Agricultural Marketing Act of 1946 (7 U.S.C. 1639p, 1639q, 1639r).
- **Numbers:** Total THC (incl. THCA) limit 0.3 percent; 10-year felony ineligibility period eliminable for industrial-hemp producers; new 5-year ineligibility for knowingly misdesignated crops.
- **Dates:** No single reauth year (structural reform).
- **Type:** REFORM.
- **Affected:** Industrial hemp (fiber/grain) producers (winners — reduced sampling/testing, eased felony bar); cannabinoid-purpose hemp growers stay under full testing; State/Tribal/USDA regulators and testing labs (must act); DEA (consulted on lab accreditation).
- **Flag:** Controversial/notable — creates a two-track hemp regime; shifts to "total THC including THCA" (a stricter chemical definition closing the THCA loophole) while simultaneously loosening field oversight for industrial hemp; eases the lifetime/10-year drug-felony bar.

### Sec. 10006 — Pilot program for the intra-organizational movement of genetically engineered microorganisms by certain authorized parties
- **Does:** Creates a new freestanding APHIS pilot. The Secretary must, within 100 days of enactment, establish a pilot authorizing not more than 75 "responsible parties" to move "covered microorganisms" (genetically engineered microorganisms that are plant pests or may pose plant pest risk; excludes select agents/toxins) in interstate commerce between their own biocontainment facilities without a permit, and to control/dispose of them. Sets responsible-party eligibility criteria (U.S. physical address; not owned/affiliated with a "country of concern" per 42 U.S.C. 19237; >1 biocontainment facility; qualified QC personnel; 3 years of prior permitted plant-pest movement; compliance capacity; no unauthorized release/material permit failure/violation in prior 5 years). Establishes a 45-day application window, first-come evaluation, approve/deny within 45 days after the window, appeal rights, supplemental applications (30-day decisions), and detailed containment/packaging requirements, recordkeeping, USDA access/inspection, anti-discrimination against GE microorganisms, quarterly reporting, 48-hour notice + 5-business-day written statement on unauthorized release, disenrollment provisions. Program terminates 3 years after selection completes; report to Congress due 6 months after termination with recommendations on permanence.
- **Amends:** New section 420A added to Subtitle A of the Plant Protection Act (7 U.S.C. 7711 et seq.). New freestanding provision (within PPA).
- **Numbers:** Cap of 75 responsible parties; 100-day establishment; 45-day application period; 45-day decision; 30-day supplemental decision; 10-day appeal window; 48-hour release notice; 5-business-day written statement; quarterly reports.
- **Dates:** Pilot terminates 3 years after application selection completes; report due 6 months after termination.
- **Type:** NEW / STUDY-OR-REPORT (pilot with congressional report).
- **Affected:** Biotech/synthetic biology firms with multiple biocontainment sites (winners — permit-free interstate movement); APHIS (administers/inspects); excludes firms tied to "countries of concern."
- **Flag:** Notable — deregulatory permit waiver for GE microorganisms; explicit national-security screen barring foreign-adversary-affiliated entities; mandates no less-favorable treatment of GE vs non-GE microorganisms.

### Sec. 10102 — Local agriculture market program
- **Does:** Amends the Local Agriculture Market Program (LAMP). Adds a statutory definition of "food hub" (business/organization actively managing aggregation, distribution, and marketing of source-identified food products to multiple buyers from multiple, primarily local/regional producers). Adds "regional food chain coordination" to collaboration; requires stakeholder consultation before and after providing grants; extends program to 2031; adds "support the purchase of special purpose equipment" as an eligible use; adds "food hub" to eligible entities. Adds outreach and technical assistance and a geographic-balance priority to application review. Creates simplified applications for entities requesting less than $100,000 with predefined project categories (direct-to-consumer: outreach/promotion, farmers market manager staff time, vendor training, planning/design, data collection/evaluation; local/regional food markets and enterprise: food hub feasibility study, regional food chain coordination staff time, technical assistance, data collection/evaluation, special purpose equipment). Updates partnership funding language from "2019 through 2023" to "2026 through 2031" supporting local/regional food system planning, implementation, regional food chain coordination, and outreach/TA/evaluation projects. Requires that not less than 10 percent and not more than 50 percent of subsection (d)(6) grant funds go to simplified-application grantees.
- **Amends:** Section 210A of the Agricultural Marketing Act of 1946 (7 U.S.C. 1627c).
- **Numbers:** Simplified application threshold < $100,000; simplified-application set-aside ≥ 10% and ≤ 50% of (d)(6) funds.
- **Dates:** 2023 → 2031; partnership funding "2026 through 2031."
- **Type:** EXTEND / REFORM.
- **Affected:** Food hubs, farmers markets, local/regional food enterprises, small applicants (winners — simplified <$100k applications, set-aside); geographically underserved regions (priority).
- **Flag:** Notable — new "food hub" statutory definition and a 10–50% funding set-aside for simplified small grants.

### Sec. 10108 — Modernization of inspection requirements
- **Does:** Rewrites the annual inspection requirement. Inspections of certified farms/handling operations must be: (A) on-site for sites located outside the United States; (B) for U.S. sites, on-site once every three years with intervening annual inspections conducted on-site OR virtually based on the operation's risk to organic integrity as determined by the Secretary; (C) for handling operations that acquire but do not physically receive/process/package/store organic products, via inspection methods including virtual that provide sufficient assurance of compliance.
- **Amends:** Section 2107(a)(5) of the Organic Foods Production Act of 1990 (7 U.S.C. 6506(a)).
- **Numbers:** On-site once every three years (U.S. operations).
- **Dates:** —
- **Type:** REFORM.
- **Affected:** Domestic certified organic operations (winners — fewer mandatory on-site inspections, virtual allowed); foreign operations still on-site annually; certifying agents (must adjust).
- **Flag:** Notable — domestic operations get on-site only once every 3 years (virtual in between) while foreign operations keep annual on-site; a risk-based loosening of inspection frequency.

### Sec. 10109 — Study and reform of National Organic Program oversight protocols
- **Does:** Adds new OFPA section 2122C. Requires the Secretary, within 12 months of enactment, to conduct a comprehensive study on whether risk-based oversight protocols and related reforms are necessary/appropriate (examining feasibility of: risk-based protocols; differential treatment of noncompliance by risk; standardized organic plans under section 2114 aligned with risk; multi-tiered certification by risk and operation scale; increased NOP guidance/interpretation). Must consider scope, scale/complexity, domestic/international location, compliance history, and other factors. Report to House/Senate Ag committees and public posting within 18 months. Consultation with the National Organic Standards Board, certifying agents, certified operations, organic consumers, and other stakeholders. Authorizes the Secretary, based on findings and after consultation with the committees, to issue regulations establishing/modifying oversight protocols (provided they maintain strong organic integrity, support a resilient domestic organic sector, and are consistent with the title), potentially to reduce oversight costs for lower-risk operations or prioritize resources for higher-risk ones. Rule of construction preserves the Secretary's enforcement authority.
- **Amends:** New section 2122C of the Organic Foods Production Act of 1990 (7 U.S.C. 6501 et seq.), inserted after 2122B.
- **Numbers:** —
- **Dates:** Study within 12 months of enactment; report within 18 months of enactment.
- **Type:** STUDY-OR-REPORT / REFORM (with grant of rulemaking authority).
- **Affected:** Lower-risk certified operations (potential winners — reduced cost/burden); NOSB, certifying agents, organic consumers (consulted).
- **Flag:** Notable — grants the Secretary new rulemaking authority to build a risk-based, multi-tiered organic oversight system off the study findings.

### Sec. 10201 — Exclusion of certain substances
- **Does:** Amends FIFRA definitions. Rewrites "plant regulator" to exclude substances intended to be produced/used within a plant; substances intended as plant nutrients/trace elements/nutritional chemicals/plant inoculants/soil amendments/vitamin hormone products; and plant biostimulants that have a low-risk profile and are of biological origin (or synthetically derived but structurally similar and functionally identical to biological-origin substances). Amends the "pest" exclusion list (subsection (hh)) to add plant biostimulant and nutritional chemical. Adds new defined terms: "plant biostimulant," "nutritional chemical," "vitamin hormone product," and "plant-incorporated protectant" (PIP, including inert ingredients per 40 C.F.R. 174.3). Rewrites FIFRA section 25(b): allows exemption by rule for pesticides adequately regulated by another agency or unnecessary to regulate; and creates an exemption for plant-incorporated protectants "resulting from endogenous genetic material found within or that could arise from the plant's gene pool" upon issuance of EPA guidance (due 1 year after enactment), with an exception where the Administrator determines regulation is necessary (via order to owner or Federal Register), and a corresponding pesticide-residue tolerance exemption under FFDCA section 408 (21 U.S.C. 346a). Conforming amendments to FIFRA section 17(c) (7 U.S.C. 136o(c)) re inert ingredients/PIP exemption.
- **Amends:** Sections 2, 25(b), and 17(c) of FIFRA (7 U.S.C. 136, 136w(b), 136o(c)); cross-references FFDCA 408 (21 U.S.C. 346a) and 40 C.F.R. parts 174.3, 174.
- **Numbers:** —
- **Dates:** EPA PIP guidance due 1 year after enactment.
- **Type:** REFORM (deregulatory — excludes substances from pesticide regulation).
- **Affected:** Plant biostimulant, nutritional chemical, vitamin hormone product makers and developers of plant-bred/endogenous-PIP crops (winners — out from under FIFRA); EPA (issues guidance/orders).
- **Flag:** Notable — broadly exempts plant biostimulants and gene-pool-derived plant-incorporated protectants from pesticide regulation, including tolerance exemption.

### Sec. 10202 — Coordination
- **Does:** Adds FIFRA section 3(i). Requires that any pesticide risk mitigation measures be developed in coordination with the Secretary of Agriculture and that EPA conduct and publish in the docket an economic analysis of implementation cost. Requires EPA to coordinate with USDA (through the Director of the Office of Pest Management Policy) to obtain and consider agronomic use data (from USDA and industry) and information on availability/economic viability of alternatives for registration/registration review and FFDCA 408 determinations, and to publish how it used (or why it did not use) USDA-provided data. Requires coordination with USDA, Interior, and Commerce on "reasonable and prudent actions and measures" arising from consultations, including feedback to Interior/Commerce on effects to end users. Permits waiver/modification of coordination for a specific action if agreed by EPA, USDA, and the registrant and published in the docket.
- **Amends:** Section 3 of FIFRA (7 U.S.C. 136a).
- **Numbers:** —
- **Dates:** —
- **Type:** REFORM.
- **Affected:** USDA/Office of Pest Management Policy (new role); pesticide registrants (winners — economic-cost analysis, alternatives consideration, waiver option); EPA (new procedural burdens).
- **Flag:** Notable — inserts USDA and economic-cost analysis into EPA pesticide risk-mitigation and ESA-related decision-making.

### Sec. 10203 — Interagency working group

Changes reporting from every 180 days to each year (and removes the 5-year period limitation). Requires reports be published on EPA's website. Rewrites the consultation provision: working group must consult (including public meetings) with industry stakeholders and NGOs at least once a year and weigh their differing interests; and before EPA implements any policy/strategy/workplan/pilot applying the Endangered Species Act to pesticide registration/review, EPA must consult covered agencies and publish input received in the docket.
- **Amends:** Section 3(c)(11) of FIFRA (7 U.S.C. 136a(c)(11)).
- **Numbers:** Reports annual (was every 180 days); consult stakeholders ≥ once/year.
- **Dates:** Removes "5-year period" limitation on reporting.
- **Type:** REFORM.
- **Affected:** Office of Pest Management Policy, industry stakeholders/NGOs (more input); EPA (transparency/consultation duties on ESA-pesticide policy).
- **Flag:** —

### Sec. 10204 — Registration review

- **Type:** EXTEND / REFORM / REPEAL (conforming repeal of PRIA 2022 §711).

### Sec. 10212 — Safe harbor for certain discharges of wildland fire chemicals
- **Does:** Bars any court from enjoining, under the Clean Water Act (33 U.S.C. 1251 et seq.), a "covered entity" from conducting aerial application of a covered fire retardant/water enhancer for wildfire suppression/control/prevention that results in a discharge, if conducted per the Feb. 16, 2023 EPA–Forest Service Federal Facility Compliance Agreement. Applies only to applications conducted before the effective date of a CWA section 402 (33 U.S.C. 1342) permit authorizing such discharge. Preserves court authority over noncompliant applications. Defines covered entity (Federal/State/local/Tribal agencies authorized for such aerial application, plus their contractors/agents) and covered fire retardant/water enhancer (evaluated, qualified, approved by the Secretary and on the current Forest Service Qualified Products List). Sunsets 5 years after enactment.
- **Amends:** New freestanding provision; operates against the Federal Water Pollution Control Act (33 U.S.C. 1251 et seq.; 1342; 1362).
- **Numbers:** —
- **Dates:** Sunsets 5 years after enactment.
- **Type:** NEW (litigation safe harbor).
- **Affected:** Forest Service / firefighting agencies and contractors (winners — shielded from injunctions); CWA plaintiffs/citizen-suit enforcement (losers).
- **Flag:** Notable/controversial — strips courts of injunction authority over aerial fire-retardant discharges pending a CWA permit.

- **Dates:** Appropriations FY2027–FY2031.
- **Type:** NEW.
- **Affected:** Biotech/synthetic biology developers, USDA agencies, EPA/FDA coordination.
- **Flag:** —

## Cross-cutting flags for this title
- No California Proposition 12 / interstate-commerce preemption provision. Despite the assignment's emphasis, this engrossed Title X contains no provision preempting state agricultural laws on production standards (e.g., a Prop 12 response). The only state-law-effect provisions are the FIFRA changes and the CWA wildfire-retardant litigation safe harbor.
- Uniform reauthorization to FY2031: Specialty Crop Block Grants, market news, urban ag, plant diagnostics, LAMP, Acer/maple, organic data, organic certification reports and funding, and FIFRA registration-review deadlines all extended from 2023 (or 2022) to 2031.
- Strong deregulatory / USDA-empowerment theme in the Regulatory Reform subtitle: excludes biostimulants, nutritional chemicals, and gene-pool-derived plant-incorporated protectants from FIFRA; mandates EPA–USDA coordination and economic-cost analysis on risk-mitigation and ESA measures; Paperwork Reduction Act waiver; and a litigation safe harbor for fire-retardant discharges.
- Organic reform cluster (Secs. 10104, 10105, 10107, 10108, 10109): builds a "risk to organic integrity" framework, allows virtual/triennial inspections for domestic operations (foreign stays annual on-site), adds organic milk data and cross-agency technical assistance, and grants the Secretary new rulemaking authority to create risk-based, multi-tiered oversight.
- Hemp two-track regime (Sec. 10005): new "only industrial hemp" designation with relaxed sampling/testing and eased drug-felony bar, paired with a stricter "total THC including THCA" standard and new lab-accreditation process (DEA-consulted).
- Biotechnology emphasis: new APHIS GE-microorganism interstate-movement pilot (cap 75; national-security screen on foreign-adversary-affiliated entities) and a new USDA Office of Biotechnology Policy ($1M/yr FY2027–FY2031).
- National-security hooks: GE-microorganism pilot bars entities owned/affiliated with a "country of concern" (per 42 U.S.C. 19237, CHIPS Act of 2022) — consistent with the bill's "National Security" title.
- Several mandates to act on tight clocks: GE-microorganism pilot within 100 days; FIFRA PIP guidance and procurement report within 1 year; organic oversight study within 12 months / report within 18 months.
- Funding figures are largely reauthorizations rather than new appropriations; the only explicit new dollar authorization in the title text is $1,000,000/year (FY2027–FY2031) for the Office of Biotechnology Policy.
